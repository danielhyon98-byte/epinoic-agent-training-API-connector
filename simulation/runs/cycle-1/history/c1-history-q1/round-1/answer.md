## Short answer

The famine of 1959 to 1961, at the heart of the Great Leap Forward (launched in 1958 and abandoned in stages during 1961 and 1962), was overwhelmingly man-made. Grain output fell by roughly a quarter between 1958 and 1960 because the people's communes, the diversion of farm labour into steel-making and water projects, and pseudo-scientific farming methods wrecked production. At the same time the state took a larger share of a smaller harvest, because officials had reported fantasy yields and the leadership believed them. The purge of Peng Dehuai at the Lushan Conference in July and August 1959 then made it politically impossible to admit the truth for another year, and 1960 became the deadliest year. Bad weather and the Soviet withdrawal of 1960 played a part, but most historians treat them as minor. Estimates of excess deaths run from about 15 to 17 million (Chinese demographers in the 1980s) to 45 million (Frank Dikötter, 2010), with most demographic studies clustering around 30 million. Both the number and the balance of blame between Mao, the system and local officials are genuinely contested.

## 1. A dated timeline you can use

- **1955 to 1957.** Collectivisation: by the end of 1956 the great majority of peasant households were in higher-stage cooperatives, where land was pooled and private ownership ended.
- **June 1957 onwards.** The Anti-Rightist Campaign followed the Hundred Flowers period. Roughly half a million people were labelled rightists. Standard textbook point: by 1958 open criticism of policy was dangerous, which helps explain why nobody stopped the Leap early.
- **November 1957.** In Moscow, Mao declared that China would overtake Britain in output of major industrial products such as steel within fifteen years (a target later shortened).
- **Winter 1957 to 1958.** Mass water-conservancy campaigns. Figures of around 100 million peasants mobilised for irrigation and dam work are commonly cited.
- **May 1958.** The second session of the Eighth Party Congress formally adopted the Great Leap Forward and the new general line calling for faster, bigger and more economical construction of socialism.
- **August 1958.** The Politburo meeting at Beidaihe decided to establish people's communes across the countryside and to double 1958 steel output to 10.7 million tonnes (1957 output was about 5.35 million tonnes).
- **Autumn 1958.** Backyard furnaces, communal mess halls and the exaggerated harvest claims all peak. Part of a genuinely good 1958 harvest was left to rot because labour had been pulled off the fields.
- **Late 1958 to spring 1959.** A limited retreat: Mao conceded some targets were unrealistic at party conferences in November and December 1958. He gave up the state chairmanship in April 1959 (Liu Shaoqi became Chairman of the People's Republic on 27 April 1959) but kept the party chairmanship and real power.
- **2 July to 16 August 1959.** The Lushan Conference (a Politburo meeting followed by a Central Committee plenum). Peng Dehuai, Minister of National Defence and a marshal, sent Mao a private letter on 14 July 1959 criticising the exaggeration and economic damage of the Leap. Mao counter-attacked on 23 July, had the letter circulated, and the plenum condemned Peng and his supporters as an anti-Party clique. Peng was dismissed as Defence Minister in September 1959 and replaced by Lin Biao. A campaign against right opportunism followed throughout the party, and the Leap was re-accelerated.
- **1960.** The worst famine year. Soviet advisers were withdrawn in July and August 1960 as the Sino-Soviet split deepened. In November 1960 the centre issued an emergency directive (the Twelve Articles) restoring some private plots and curbing commune excesses.
- **1961.** The retreat proper: the Sixty Articles on agriculture, the closing of the mess halls, smaller communes with accounting pushed down to the production team, and the first large grain imports (around 5 to 6 million tonnes in 1961, mostly from Canada and Australia).
- **11 January to 7 February 1962.** The Seven Thousand Cadres Conference in Beijing (see section 6). The 1962 harvest recovered to about 160 million tonnes.

## 2. The policies and how each one cut food supply

Think of these as two groups: things that reduced the harvest, and things that increased how much of it the state took away.

**Reducing the harvest**

1. **People's communes (from August 1958).** Cooperatives were merged into about 26,000 communes by the end of 1958, averaging roughly 5,000 households each and covering almost all rural households. Private plots were abolished, and the commune became both the unit of farming and a unit of military-style labour mobilisation. Economists such as Justin Yifu Lin (in an article published in 1990) argued that the decisive damage was the loss of the right to leave the collective: once peasants could not exit, there was no incentive to work hard and no way to discipline shirkers.
2. **Communal mess halls.** Communes fed members in public dining halls, and in late 1958 many offered unlimited free food. Standard textbook point: this encouraged overconsumption and waste in autumn 1958 and ran down stocks before the first poor harvest. The mess halls were only abolished in 1961.
3. **Backyard furnaces (autumn 1958).** To hit the 10.7 million tonne steel target, tens of millions of people (figures of up to 90 million are cited for late 1958) built small furnaces, melting down pots, tools and even door hinges. Reported 1958 steel output was around 11 million tonnes, but roughly 3 million tonnes of that was unusable pig iron. The real cost was agricultural: labour was taken from the fields during the 1958 harvest, and trees were felled for fuel.
4. **Pseudo-scientific farming methods.** Mao's Eight-Point Charter for agriculture, influenced by the Soviet agronomist Trofim Lysenko's ideas, pushed close planting (seedlings sown far too densely, so they shaded and starved one another) and deep ploughing (sometimes to a metre or more, which destroyed topsoil structure). Badly engineered irrigation projects also waterlogged and salinised land in parts of the north.
5. **The Four Pests campaign (1958).** The mass killing of sparrows, alongside rats, flies and mosquitoes, removed a predator of insects; locust and other insect plagues followed, and in 1960 bed bugs replaced sparrows on the list.

**Increasing what the state took**

6. **The wind of exaggeration.** Under political pressure, provinces and counties reported fantasy yields, with so-called sputnik fields claiming grain per unit of land many times any realistic figure. The 1958 harvest was announced at about 375 million tonnes; the later official figure is about 200 million tonnes (a real but modest rise on 1957's 195 million).
7. **Procurement.** Because the state believed the inflated figures, it set grain procurement quotas against a harvest that did not exist. Procurement rose from about 48 million tonnes in 1957 to about 67 million tonnes in 1959, which was close to 40 per cent of the real 1959 harvest, against a norm of around a quarter. Peasants who could not deliver were accused of hiding grain and punished.
8. **Exports and the cities.** China continued to export grain in 1959 (about 4 million tonnes) and 1960, partly to pay for Soviet equipment and partly to protect its international reputation. Urban residents were rationed; the countryside was left to absorb the shortfall. The historian Felix Wemheuer (in a comparative study published in 2014) stresses this urban-rural divide as a key feature of how the famine was distributed.

**The Lushan link.** Peng's purge matters in a causation essay because it explains timing: the harvest was already falling in 1959, but after Lushan admitting shortages was politically suicidal, so quotas, mess halls and exaggeration continued into 1960, the year with the highest death rate.

Other checkable points:

- Official statistics show China's population falling by about 10 million in 1960, which even the Party's own official history published in 2011 acknowledges.
- The famine was regional, not uniform. The worst-hit provinces were Sichuan, Anhui, Henan, Gansu and Guizhou, several of them run by radical provincial leaders (Li Jingquan in Sichuan, Zeng Xisheng in Anhui, Wu Zhipu in Henan). Official provincial figures give Anhui a death rate of roughly 68 per 1,000 in 1960.
- The Xinyang incident in Henan (1959 to 1960) is the best-documented local catastrophe: around a million deaths in one prefecture, with local officials beating and torturing peasants accused of hoarding and sealing off villages to stop people fleeing. Yang Jisheng, who was a journalist for the state news agency, opens his book Tombstone with Xinyang.
- Violence was part of the death toll. Dikötter's reading of provincial archives led him to argue that at least 2.5 million people were beaten or tortured to death and that one to three million killed themselves. Those sub-figures are his estimates, and other scholars treat them with caution.

**Flag for your essay:** there is no settled figure, but there is a defensible range. The demographic studies of the 1980s cluster between about 17 and 30 million; the archive-based histories of the 2000s run from about 36 to 45 million; the lowest Chinese estimates and the highest journalistic ones are outliers that most scholars reject. The famine historian Cormac Ó Gráda, in a review essay published in 2011, questioned Dikötter's 45 million as too high and favoured estimates nearer the demographers' range.

## 5. Why the estimates differ (so you can explain the disagreement, not just list it)

1. **Which baseline death rate is assumed.** Excess deaths means deaths above what would have happened anyway. If you assume 1957's rate of about 10.8 per 1,000 as normal, you get a higher excess than if you assume the rate would have been higher.
2. **How much to correct the data.** Deaths were under-registered in the late 1950s, and the 1953 and 1964 censuses have known gaps. Banister corrects upwards; Chinese official demographers correct less.
3. **Whether lost births count.** Ashton's group and Yang Jisheng separate deaths from births that did not happen; some popular figures merge the two into a single population loss, which inflates the number.
4. **Which years count.** Some studies use 1959 to 1961; Dikötter uses 1958 to 1962, which adds deaths from overwork and violence before and after the peak.
5. **Method.** Demographers model national data; Dikötter and Yang extrapolate from the provinces whose archives they could reach, and critics argue those provinces may not be typical.

## 6. The 1962 meeting and the admission that it was man-made

At the Seven Thousand Cadres Conference (11 January to 7 February 1962), the largest gathering of officials the Party had held, Liu Shaoqi, Chairman of the People's Republic, gave the famous assessment. Paraphrased: he said that the disaster was about three-tenths natural and seven-tenths man-made, attributing the phrase to peasants he had spoken to in his home province of Hunan during a visit in 1961. Mao made a limited self-criticism as the person ultimately responsible, but the conference left the system itself unexamined. Many historians argue that Mao's resentment of Liu's verdict fed into the Cultural Revolution: Liu was purged in 1966 to 1967 and died in custody in 1969.

## 7. Where historians actually disagree about blame

**Mao personally.**
- Dikötter (2010) places responsibility squarely on Mao: he argues the archives show Mao knew of mass deaths by 1959 and still insisted on procurement and exports. Dikötter also cites remarks Mao made at a meeting in Shanghai in March 1959 which, in Dikötter's reading, show Mao accepting that many would die. Other scholars argue that remark concerned cutting industrial projects rather than people, and that Dikötter reads it too literally. This is a live dispute, so present it as one.
- Thomas Bernstein, in a 1984 article, argued that unlike Stalin in Ukraine in 1932 to 1933, Mao did not intend to starve peasants; the deaths came from excessive procurement based on false reports. In a later article (2006), Bernstein shifted the emphasis to Mao's wilful refusal to act on the evidence after Lushan.
- Roderick MacFarquhar, in the second volume of his study of the origins of the Cultural Revolution (1983), treats the Leap as elite politics: Mao's ambition and the fear of his colleagues, with Lushan as the moment when correction became impossible.
- Jung Chang and Jon Halliday (2005) go furthest, presenting Mao as knowingly exporting food while people starved; academic reviewers generally judged this account one-sided.

**The system.**
- Yang Jisheng blames Mao but argues the deeper cause was a totalitarian system in which nobody could tell the truth upwards and nobody below could refuse orders.
- The economist Amartya Sen, writing from the 1980s onwards, used China as the main example of his argument that major famines do not occur in functioning democracies with a free press, because information and political pressure force governments to act. On this view the absence of any feedback, not Mao's personality, was decisive.
- Lin and Dennis Tao Yang (in an article published in 2000) found that the famine's severity across provinces was explained by both the fall in output and the state's urban-biased procurement, meaning the design of the system distributed the hunger onto the countryside.

**Local cadres.**
- Ralph Thaxton's village study (2008) of Da Fo in Henan shows local officials using violence, food deprivation and forced labour, with peasants responding by hiding grain, eating unripe crops and stealing. On this view the famine was experienced as cadre coercion as much as central policy.
- Dikötter's archives also document local brutality, and Yang Jisheng's Xinyang chapter shows provincial and county leaders (Wu Zhipu in Henan) outbidding each other in radicalism. The debate is whether cadres were the cause or the transmission belt: were they acting on their own, or responding rationally to a system that punished honesty and rewarded exaggeration?

**The weather and the Soviets.**
- The official Chinese explanation for decades was three years of natural disasters, plus the Soviet withdrawal of July and August 1960. There was genuine drought and flooding in parts of China in 1959 to 1961, so the claim is not invented.
- Most Western scholars and many Chinese ones give weather a minor role, pointing out that output had already fallen sharply in 1959 and that the regions with the worst deaths were not always the regions with the worst weather. Some economists who have modelled agricultural output give weather a real but minority share of the fall. Liu Shaoqi's own three-tenths figure is sometimes cited as the Party's own concession that policy was the larger cause.
- The Soviet withdrawal hit industry more than farming and came in mid-1960, after the famine had begun, so most historians treat it as an aggravating factor, not a cause.
