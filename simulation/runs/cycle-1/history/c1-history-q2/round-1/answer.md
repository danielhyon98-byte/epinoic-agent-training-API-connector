## (a) Value and limitations of Source A [4 marks]

Analysed as the examiner would want, that is, as if Lindemann and his diary were real.

**Origin and purpose.** Source A is a private diary entry written in Berlin on 9 March 1936, two days after German troops entered the demilitarised Rhineland, by a junior official inside the German Foreign Office. For a historian asking why Britain and France did not resist, that origin has real value. It is contemporaneous; it records what Foreign Minister Neurath was telling his staff that morning; and because a private diary has no public audience, the writer has no reason to disguise German weakness. He admits that the force was small and that a French advance would have forced a retreat. That matters for the question: it is German evidence that resistance was feasible, so Anglo-French inaction has to be explained as a choice rather than an impossibility. The same origin limits it. The writer is junior and much of what he records is hearsay: Neurath "told us this morning", he knows Hitler's state of mind only because "I am told", and the British peer "is reported to have said" the back-garden remark. He is writing on 9 March, before the French foreign minister had been to London and before the League Council had met there the following week, so his confident "they will not march" is a prediction, not knowledge of the Allied decisions. Diarists also write partly to show themselves well informed, which is one reason to treat the ministry gossip he records with care.

**Content.** The content is valuable for the German reading of Allied intentions: that "The British are the key", that Paris would "go to London rather than to the frontier", and that the French electoral timetable mattered. These are the factors historians weigh, and the source shows Berlin counting on them at the time. Its limitations are those of perspective and tone. A German official can report Berlin's guesses about London and Paris, but not the British Cabinet's view of its own military weakness, nor the French General Staff's insistence that any response required general mobilisation. The source therefore cannot explain the Allied decision from the inside; the one explanation it offers for France, the April elections and a treaty "half abandoned", is the diarist's inference. The mocking tone ("Let them", the joke that "Locarno died in its own bed and nobody sent flowers") suggests a triumphant mood in which Allied resolve is sneered at rather than assessed.

How this is built: every value and every limitation is tied to a specific feature of the source (its date, the writer's rank, a quoted phrase, an omission) and to the stated question. Generic points such as "it is only one person's opinion" or "it is an extract" earn little.

## (b) Fact-check: where each claim comes from

I'm working from the source and from memory, with no way to look anything up, so I've marked each claim as solid, contested, hearsay or invented, and said what kind of evidence it rests on. Where I'm not certain of a figure or an attribution, I say so.

### 1. "Nineteen thousand soldiers and some police"

The figures you've seen are not rival answers to the same question. They count different things. In the accounts I know (Shirer's history of 1960, Kershaw's Hitler biography of 1998, and the standard textbook treatment):

- **About 3,000** is the number who actually crossed the Rhine to the western edge of the zone: three battalions, sent to Aachen, Trier and Saarbrücken. This is the figure that dramatises the gamble, and it is the one Shirer stressed.
- **About 22,000** is the regular army force that entered the demilitarised zone as a whole, usually described as nineteen infantry battalions with artillery. Most of it stayed east of the Rhine.
- **About 30,000 or more** is that force plus the Landespolizei, the state police units already stationed in the Rhineland, which were formally absorbed into the army during the operation. That is the "some police" in the source.
- **"Nineteen thousand"** I do not recognise from any standard account. Nineteen does appear, but as the number of infantry battalions. Whether or not your teacher intended it, it is a neat example of a true number attached to the wrong unit becoming a false claim.
- **"a hundred thousand"** in the Paris papers: I cannot verify what the Paris press printed. What is well documented is that French military intelligence enormously overestimated German strength by counting police, SA and other paramilitary formations, and reported figures to the French government that ran into the hundreds of thousands. I am not certain of the exact figure, so check it in your textbook before you use it.

Verdict: the total is a matter of definition rather than genuine disagreement. What every account agrees on, and what matters for the question, is that the force was small, lightly equipped, and under orders to fall back towards the Rhine if the French attacked. That last point rests on the German army's own operational orders, captured after the war and used at Nuremberg, and on General Jodl's testimony there in 1946 that the French covering forces could have overwhelmed the German troops.

### 2. "tails between our legs": did Hitler say it, and how do we know?

Where it comes from: Paul Schmidt, Hitler's interpreter, in memoirs published after the war (in German in 1949, in English in 1951). Schmidt records Hitler saying, looking back, that the forty-eight hours after the march into the Rhineland were the most nerve-racking of his life, and that if the French had marched the Germans would have had to withdraw in humiliation because their forces were wholly inadequate. Shirer quoted Schmidt in 1960, and the remark has appeared in textbooks ever since, usually without saying where it comes from.

So "how do we know" has an honest answer: not very securely. The quotation is a recollection, written more than a decade after the event, by a man who after 1945 had every reason to present himself as a sober professional watching a reckless gambler, and it reports a remark made in hindsight, not on the weekend itself. As far as I know, no document from March 1936 records Hitler saying it. What is solid is the substance behind it, because it is corroborated independently: the withdrawal orders; Jodl's Nuremberg testimony; and Blomberg's documented nervousness as War Minister, who wanted the forward battalions pulled back when French troop movements were reported. Jodl, too, was testifying after the fact and had an interest in showing that the army had not been an aggressor, which is why the orders themselves are the best evidence of all.

So: "Hitler feared French resistance and the army was ready to retreat" is a well-supported fact. "Hitler said these exact words" rests on one post-war memoir.

What the source does with it is the "suspiciously famous" trap your teacher means. It puts the remark into Hitler's mouth on the weekend, said to Neurath, which turns a hindsight quotation into a contemporaneous one. A diarist in Berlin on 9 March 1936 could not have read Schmidt's memoir, and the detail that Hitler "did not sleep on Saturday night" is an invented dramatisation of the same later remark. Compare a different famous Rhineland quotation: Hitler's remark that he went the way Providence dictated with the assurance of a sleepwalker, from a public speech in Munich on 14 March 1936 during the campaign for the plebiscite of 29 March. That one was reported in the press at the time and is documented. Two famous quotations, two very different kinds of provenance; the exam skill is telling them apart.

### 3. Who was the lord with the "back garden" comment?

The remark that the Germans were only going into their own back garden is conventionally attributed to Lord Lothian (Philip Kerr, Marquess of Lothian), a Liberal peer who was not in government in 1936, was known for thinking Versailles unjust to Germany, and later served as British ambassador in Washington from 1939 until his death in 1940. I am confident of the attribution as it appears in the standard literature. I am less sure of the exact occasion and wording: the remark is usually quoted without a precise primary reference, as something said in conversation or in a letter rather than on the record in Parliament. So the "who" is reasonably solid; the exact words are a textbook commonplace, not a documented speech. Notice that the source itself hedges with "is reported to have said", which is honest, and exactly the kind of phrase you should pick out when evaluating a source.

What the remark stands for is well documented. The British government and much of British opinion treated the move as the recovery of German territory rather than an act of aggression. Anthony Eden, Foreign Secretary since December 1935, told the Commons on Monday 9 March that Britain condemned the unilateral breach of Locarno, would honour its Locarno obligations if France or Belgium were actually attacked, but saw no reason to think the German action threatened hostilities, and that the matter should go to the League Council and the Locarno powers. The leading article in The Times on the same day treated the move as an opening for negotiation. Behind this lay Britain's military unreadiness, the distraction of the Abyssinian crisis, and the fact, a standard point in the specialist literature, that Eden's Foreign Office had already been treating the demilitarised zone as a bargaining counter in early 1936.

### 4. Did the French not act because of their elections?

Separate the facts from the inference.

Facts: France was governed in March 1936 by a caretaker ministry under Albert Sarraut, with Pierre-Étienne Flandin as Foreign Minister, in office since January and facing elections on 26 April and 3 May. On 8 March Sarraut broadcast that France would not negotiate while Strasbourg lay under German guns (a paraphrase, not his exact words), but within days the government had decided to refer the breach to the League Council and seek British backing rather than act alone. General Gamelin, Chief of the General Staff, told ministers that any military response required general mobilisation, that is, calling up reservists, and the Finance Ministry and Bank of France warned of the consequences for the franc. Britain made clear it would not support military action. Flandin's visit to London the following week produced only a League condemnation (19 March) and promises of staff talks.

So the diarist is right that the electoral timetable was a real constraint; every account of the crisis includes it. But "Their elections come in April" as the explanation is his inference, delivered as certainty, by a man who could not know the French Council's deliberations and who ignores the military, financial and British factors. It is a true fact used as a monocausal opinion.

Where historians disagree:

- The traditional account, and most textbooks, stress a failure of nerve: a weak caretaker government, electoral timidity, British restraint.
- A. J. P. Taylor argued in his 1961 book on the origins of the war that the French never intended to act and went to London expecting, even hoping for, a British refusal they could blame; the episode, in his view, exposed France's defensive posture more than British obstruction.
- Stephen Schuker, in an article published in 1986 drawing on French financial and military records, argued that French inaction was structural: the franc crisis, the army's mobilisation system and its defensive doctrine meant France had in effect written off the zone before 7 March. On this reading the election was a convenient excuse rather than the cause.
- J. T. Emmerson's detailed diplomatic study of 1977 treats the crisis as multilateral, giving weight to British policy that had already concluded the zone was negotiable.
- Ian Kershaw's 1998 biography stresses the German side: a gamble that worked because Hitler took it at the moment the Western powers were least able to respond.

Your diarist's "half abandoned" treaty has a kernel of truth: neither power had acted against German conscription in March 1935, and British thinking had already begun to write the zone off. His phrasing, though, "no Frenchman will die for a treaty", echoes a famous French anti-war headline of May 1939 asking whether France should die for Danzig (Marcel Déat), three years in the future. Another suspiciously famous line, this time by anachronism.
