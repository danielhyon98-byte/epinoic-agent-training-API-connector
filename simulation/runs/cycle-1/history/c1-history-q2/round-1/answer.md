## (a) Value and limitations of Source A for the stated enquiry

**Value**

- **Origin.** A private diary kept by a junior official inside the German Foreign Office, written in Berlin on Monday 9 March 1936, two days after the troops moved. It is contemporary rather than a post-war recollection, and it was not written for anyone else to read, so the writer has little reason to pose. For a historian asking why Britain and France did not resist, that gives direct evidence of how the German side read Western intentions while the outcome was still open: that Paris would "appeal to the League and go to London rather than to the frontier", and that "The British are the key". The German reading proved right, and the diary records it before anyone knew that.
- **Content.** The entry shows the gap between the real German force and what the Western side believed: "Nineteen thousand soldiers and some police" against Paris reports of "a hundred thousand", plus Hitler's and Blomberg's reported readiness to pull back if a French division moved. Western overestimation of German strength is a standard part of the explanation for French inaction, and the source gives an insider's view of how thin the bluff was. The remark of "one of their lords" about a "back garden" also captures the British attitude that the Rhineland was German territory anyway, a documented reason why London refused to back force.

**Limitations**

- **Origin.** The diarist is a junior man in Berlin, not in London or Paris. Everything he says about British and French motives is second-hand ("I am told", "is reported to have said") or his own inference. He cannot know what the British Cabinet, the French General Staff or the French Finance Ministry were advising. For this enquiry the source reports the German view of Western motives, not the Western motives themselves.
- **Purpose.** A private diary written two days into a successful gamble, in a mocking, triumphant mood ("the joke is" that Locarno "died in its own bed"; "Let them"). It has no reason to be balanced, and it was written before the French Foreign Minister's visit to London and the League Council meeting in mid-March, so it cannot explain how the crisis actually ended. The confident claim that "they will not march" because "Their elections come in April" is the diarist's explanation presented as fact.
- **Content.** The troop figure depends on who is counted (see part (b)), and a Ministry insider has reason to minimise the operation. The source is silent on the factors historians now stress on the French side, above all the General Staff's insistence on general mobilisation and the state of French finances.

Compressing this: a 4-mark answer typically needs two or three points of value and two or three of limitation, each tied explicitly to origin, purpose or content and to the stated enquiry; no conclusion is needed. That is the common expectation, not the official markscheme, which I don't have.

## (b) Fact-check: what is solid, what is contested, and where each claim comes from

I can't look anything up in this session, so I'm working from memory. Where I'm unsure I say so, and the Sources line at the end lists the works I'm recalling rather than consulting.

**1. The date and the day. Solid.** 7 March 1936 was a Saturday, so "Saturday night" and a Monday entry dated 9 March fit; any perpetual calendar will confirm it. Hitler announced the move in the Reichstag that same day, claiming that the Franco-Soviet Pact, ratified by the French Chamber of Deputies on 27 February 1936, had already broken Locarno, and he paired the breach with an offer of new non-aggression pacts. Standard textbook content.

**2. Neurath and Blomberg. Names solid; the nerves rest on post-war evidence.** Konstantin von Neurath was Foreign Minister (1932 to 1938) and Werner von Blomberg was War Minister (1933 to 1938). That Blomberg lost his nerve and wanted the troops "back on the east bank" is widely repeated and probably true, but notice where it comes from: testimony at Nuremberg (General Alfred Jodl in 1946 described the German forces as far too weak to resist) and the generals' memoirs. Those witnesses had every reason after 1945 to stress their own caution and Hitler's recklessness. Treat the detail as well supported but not neutral. The best evidence that a withdrawal was contemplated is the army's own operational orders, captured after the war, which provided for falling back if the French attacked; check your textbook for how they are described. The crossing at Cologne is well attested by photographs of the day; I can't vouch for Mainz as a crossing point.

**3. Locarno "died in its own bed". The joke is invented; the point behind it is solid.** By the Locarno treaties of October 1925, Germany, France and Belgium accepted their western frontiers and Germany reaffirmed the demilitarised Rhineland (created by the Treaty of Versailles in 1919 and covering the left bank plus a 50 km strip east of the river), with Britain and Italy as guarantors. Neither guarantor acted in 1936. Italy was by then at odds with Britain and France over its invasion of Abyssinia (begun October 1935, with League sanctions in force), one reason the Locarno system was already failing.

**4. The British ambassador and the League. Solid in substance.** The ambassador in Berlin was Sir Eric Phipps; I don't know of a documented remark of his in these words, but the line matches British policy. Foreign Secretary Anthony Eden (in office since December 1935) told the House of Commons on 9 March 1936 that Britain condemned the breach, saw no threat of hostilities in it, would honour its Locarno obligations if France or Belgium were attacked, and wanted the matter taken to the League (my paraphrase, not his words). The reasons historians give: British rearmament had barely begun and the Chiefs of Staff warned against war; the Abyssinian entanglement; a widespread feeling that Versailles had been too harsh and that the Rhineland was German land; and the hope of a general settlement with Germany, for which, as several historians note, the Foreign Office had begun in early 1936 to consider the zone as a bargaining counter. The League Council, meeting in London, found Germany in breach on 19 March 1936, and the matter passed into negotiations that led nowhere.

**5. The lord and the "back garden". Attributed to Lord Lothian; the original record is the weak link.** Textbooks credit the remark to Lord Lothian (Philip Kerr), a Liberal peer who held no government office in 1936, thought Versailles unjust to Germany, and served as ambassador to the United States from 1939 until his death in 1940. In paraphrase, he is said to have remarked that the Germans had only walked into their own back garden. I can't tell you the document in which it was first recorded, whether a letter, a conversation reported by a journalist or something else, and textbooks rarely footnote it, which is exactly your teacher's point. The diary's "is reported to have said" is the honest form. In an essay, use it as an emblem of one strand of British opinion, not as a statement of government policy.

**6. "Nineteen thousand soldiers" and "a hundred thousand". Depends on who is counted.** Your three figures are not rival answers to the same question; they count different things:

- about 3,000: the troops (three battalions) that actually crossed the Rhine to the west bank, heading for Aachen, Trier and Saarbrücken; this is the figure William Shirer, an American correspondent in Berlin at the time, made famous in his 1960 history;
- around 22,000: the regular army force that entered the demilitarised zone as a whole, most of which stayed east of the Rhine because the zone included the strip on that bank;
- around 30,000 or more: that force plus roughly 14,000 men of the Rhineland's state police, who were placed under army command during the operation, which is the "some police" of the diary.

I'm confident about the structure of the discrepancy; the exact totals differ by a few thousand between accounts. 19,000 is not a figure I recognise as standard. I have seen the force described as nineteen infantry battalions, which may be where the number comes from, but I'm not certain of that, so ask your teacher where she took it from. The "a hundred thousand" claim is a plausible invention: French military intelligence did produce estimates several times the real figure by counting police, SA and other paramilitary formations, and those estimates are part of why General Maurice Gamelin, the French Chief of the General Staff, told ministers that any response required general mobilisation. I don't recall what the Paris press printed, so treat the exact figure as fiction.

**7. "tails between our legs". Real in substance, but retrospective, second-hand and moved.** The saying comes from the post-war memoir of Paul Schmidt, Hitler's interpreter, published in German in 1949 and in English soon after. Schmidt recalled Hitler saying, looking back, that the forty-eight hours after the march were the most nerve-racking of his life and that a French advance would have forced a humiliating withdrawal because German forces were far too weak (my paraphrase; the English words you know are a translation, popularised by Shirer in 1960). So it is one man's recollection, written after Hitler's defeat by someone who had served him; the exact wording cannot be verified; and Schmidt reports it as Hitler's later reflection, not as something said to Neurath that weekend. As far as I know, no document from March 1936 records Hitler saying it; the one contemporary private record from inside the regime that I know of is Goebbels's diary, published in full only decades later, and I don't remember its wording for that weekend, so don't quote it on my say-so. Your teacher has transplanted a hindsight remark into a contemporaneous diary, which tests whether you notice that a 9 March entry cannot contain a line first recorded a decade later; the detail that Hitler "did not sleep on Saturday night" is a dramatisation of the same remark. For contrast, Hitler's remark that he went the way Providence dictated with the assurance of a sleepwalker (paraphrase) comes from a public speech in Munich on 14 March 1936, reported in the press at the time: famous, but documented. Two famous lines, two very different kinds of provenance; "suspiciously famous" is a fair label for any line that appears in every textbook without a footnote.

**8. The French elections and "no Frenchman will die for a treaty". The election is fact; the explanation is the diarist's opinion, and historians weigh it differently.** France held general elections on 26 April and 3 May 1936, won by the Popular Front, which brought Léon Blum to power in June. In March the government was Albert Sarraut's, in office since January, with Pierre-Étienne Flandin as Foreign Minister. Sarraut said in a radio broadcast on 8 March that France would not leave Strasbourg exposed to German guns (paraphrase), but no French troops moved. The reasons historians give, roughly in order of weight: Gamelin's advice that action required general mobilisation, backed by inflated estimates of German strength; the British refusal to support force, which Flandin met when he went to London in the second week of March; the fragile state of French finances and the franc, which made mobilisation look unaffordable; and the reluctance of a caretaker ministry to call up reservists weeks before polling, which is where the diarist's point fits. So the election is one real consideration, not the whole answer, and "no Frenchman will die for a treaty" is the diarist's inference about public feeling, delivered as certainty. It also echoes a famous headline by Marcel Déat in May 1939 asking whether France should die for Danzig: an anachronism of three years, and another line that is famous for a different occasion.

On the debate. Churchill's memoir of 1948 presented the Rhineland as a missed chance to stop Hitler cheaply. A. J. P. Taylor's 1961 book argued that the French never meant to act and went to London half hoping for a British refusal they could blame, and used episodes like this to present Hitler as an opportunist exploiting Western weakness rather than a planner following a blueprint. Stephen Schuker argued in an article of 1986 that French inaction was structural: the franc crisis, the mobilisation system and a defensive doctrine meant France had in effect written off the zone before 7 March, so the election was an excuse rather than a cause. Ian Kershaw's 1998 biography of Hitler stresses the German side: a gamble that worked because it was taken at the moment the Western powers were least able to respond. "half abandoned" is a judgement, but a defensible one: neither power had acted when Germany reintroduced conscription in March 1935, and British officials had begun to treat the zone as negotiable.

**9. "Paris will appeal to the League and go to London rather than to the frontier". Solid.** That is what the French government did (points 4 and 8).
