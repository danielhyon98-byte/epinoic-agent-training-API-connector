## 1. Testing the Company's three claims

The tool for all three is the same:

percentage change = (later value − earlier value) ÷ earlier value × 100

Two decisions come first: which baseline year you use (2016 is the first row; 2018 is the last row before the autumn 2019 breach), and what the claim would need to be true. Say which baseline you chose in your answer, because the Company doesn't.

**Claim 1: saltmarsh "has grown sixfold". This holds.**
- 2016 to 2024: 74 ÷ 12 = 6.17, so about 6.2 times.
- As a percentage change: (74 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 = 62 ÷ 12 × 100 = 516.7 per cent, about 517 per cent.
- 2018 to 2024: 74 ÷ 11 = 6.73, nearly sevenfold; (74 − 11) ÷ 11 × 100 = 572.7 per cent.
- Either baseline supports "sixfold". Note that it is sixfold from a small base: the absolute gain is 62 ha.

**Claim 2: properties flooded have "fallen by 85 per cent". This holds from 2016, only just.**
- 2016 to 2024: (41 − 6) ÷ 41 × 100 = 35 ÷ 41 × 100 = 85.4 per cent fall.
- 2018 to 2024: (38 − 6) ÷ 38 × 100 = 32 ÷ 38 × 100 = 84.2 per cent fall.
- So "85 per cent" is accurate from 2016 and a touch generous from 2018. Had the Company used 2022 (4 properties), the fall would be (41 − 4) ÷ 41 × 100 = 90.2 per cent, so it did not pick the most flattering end year here.

**Claim 3: the maintenance bill has "more than halved". This does not hold; you are right.**
- "More than halved" means the 2024 figure must be below half the starting figure. Half of £820 thousand is £410 thousand; half of £870 thousand (the 2018 peak) is £435 thousand. The 2024 figure is £560 thousand, above both.
- Actual change, 2016 to 2024: (560 − 820) ÷ 820 × 100 = −260 ÷ 820 × 100 = −31.7 per cent, a fall of just under a third. Put another way, 560 ÷ 820 × 100 = 68.3 per cent, so the bill is still about two thirds of its 2016 level.
- Using the most generous baseline, the 2018 peak: (560 − 870) ÷ 870 × 100 = −310 ÷ 870 × 100 = −35.6 per cent; 2024 is 560 ÷ 870 × 100 = 64.4 per cent of 2018.
- So on the Company's own table the fall is roughly a third, not more than a half. That is a clean, checkable contradiction between the text and the data, and it belongs in (b) as well as (a).

Tally: two claims hold on the Company's own numbers; the one about money, which carries the recommendation, does not.

## 2. The rest of the number work, for you to do

Same formula. The places I would look, and the question to ask at each:

1. **Properties flooded, 2022 to 2024.** The figure rises from 4 to 6. Work out the percentage change, then ask whether a change of two properties is meaningful, and what else in the table changed in those two years. Percentage changes on tiny bases always look dramatic.
2. **Wave height against flooding.** Work out the change in mean winter wave height from 2020 to 2024 and from 2016 to 2024. Then set 2016 (1.1 m, 41 properties) beside 2024 (1.4 m, 6 properties): what does it say about the scheme if flooding stays low in rougher winters? Then look at the 2020 row, where the lowest wave height in the table coincides with the sharpest fall in flooding, in the year the gauge was offline for eleven weeks. That row is both the scheme's weakest evidence and, with 2022 and 2024, part of its strongest; make sure you see both sides.
3. **Before the breach, 2016 to 2018.** All four columns moved before the scheme existed. Work out the direction and size of each change; that tells you whether 2016 is a fair "before" and whether saltmarsh was already being lost.
4. **Saltmarsh against the flooded area.** 95 ha was flooded; 74 ha is now saltmarsh. Work out the share, then compute the gain in each two-year step from 2018 onwards and describe what is happening to the rate of growth.
5. **Money.** Compare £6.2 million with £19 million: what fraction, and what saving? Then take the annual maintenance saving (2016 bill minus 2024 bill, in £ thousand) and divide £6,200 thousand by it. The result is the number of years the saving would take to repay the scheme, which puts "better option" in perspective.

For "analyse" at 4 marks the examiner wants relationships, not five separate descriptions: link the 2019 breach to the turning point in every column, link saltmarsh growth to flooding (a wide intertidal surface absorbs wave energy and gives water room to spread), name one thing that does not fit, and remember that the loss of 95 ha of farmland is an effect too, even if the source calls it "low-grade farmland" and keeps it out of the table.

## 3. What a complete answer to (b) needs

I don't have your teacher's markscheme, so this is my reading of what a 6-mark "evaluate" question rewards: strengths and weaknesses of the source as evidence, each tied to a specific detail from it, and a judgement of degree at the end. "Reliability" here has three layers, and a top answer keeps them apart: are the numbers trustworthy, do they support the claims made about them, and do they support the recommendation for North Quay. The points below are the raw material; the selection, argument and weighing are yours.

**Who produced it and why**
- Harrowmouth Harbour Company operates the port and is recommending that a £19 million revetment be cancelled. It has a financial and reputational interest in the realignment looking good; "The results speak for themselves" is the language of advocacy, not monitoring.
- The £19 million is the Company's own estimate for a scheme never built, so the cost comparison is realignment's actual cost against hard engineering's forecast cost.
- It is a company review, not an independent report by a regulator, a university or an environment agency, and nothing says anyone else checked it.

**How the data were collected**
- The note says the figures were "compiled by the Company from its own tide gauge and from the Port Harrow Residents' Association flood log". The physical data come from the beneficiary; the flooding data are self-reported by residents. No method at all is given for saltmarsh area or maintenance costs.
- A flood log depends on who reports, how consistently, and what counts as "flooded". The column header does not say whether "properties flooded" means in that year, over two years, or per event.
- The 2020 wave figure was "partly estimated because the gauge was offline for eleven weeks". A winter is about thirteen weeks, so most of that winter's mean is an estimate, in the first post-breach year and the year with the sharpest fall in flooding. A tide gauge also records water level rather than wave height, so it is fair to ask how wave height was measured at all.
- Give credit where due: the Company disclosed the gauge problem. A source that admits a gap is partly more trustworthy, not less.

**What the data can and cannot show**
- Five rows at two-year intervals, three of them after the breach: a short, gappy record in which trend and year-to-year weather are hard to separate.
- Maintenance is for the whole estuary, so a fall there cannot be attributed to Saltmarsh Point alone; other works, new contracts or deferred repairs could explain it.
- Correlation is not cause: flooding fell when the scheme opened, but 2020 was also the calmest winter in the table. The 2022 and 2024 rows (rougher winters, still low flooding) are the source's best evidence that the scheme works, and a balanced answer says so.
- Small numbers: 4 and 6 properties. Percentages built on them swing wildly.
- "Saltmarsh" is not defined. Real saltmarsh vegetation takes years to establish, so if the table counts all intertidal area, "sixfold" means something different.
- The text contradicts its own table on the maintenance bill; your working from section 1 is the evidence.

**What is missing**
- Any data about North Quay itself: what lies behind the wall, its value, its wave exposure, its residents. The recommendation transfers one site's result to another.
- Costs the Company does not count: the 95 ha of farmland, who owned it and whether they were compensated, and whether a new set-back defence had to be built and maintained. Realignment normally needs one, and the source is silent.
- Any voice except the Company's: farmers, North Quay residents, the Residents' Association, environmental bodies.
- Any control: a similar estuary or stretch of coast with no scheme over the same years.
- Any long-term effect: sediment movement, erosion elsewhere in the estuary, how the marsh will cope with rising sea level.
- A definition of "better": cheaper for the Company, safer for residents, better for habitat, or better over fifty years? Better for whom is the question that turns a list into an evaluation.

**The judgement**
State a degree and make it depend on purpose. For instance: the table is moderately useful evidence that flooding in Port Harrow fell after the breach and that saltmarsh grew; the Company's commentary is unreliable, because one claim is wrong and the interest is plain; and the source is weak evidence for the North Quay recommendation, because it contains no data about North Quay and comes from the party that would save £19 million. One sentence on what would make it more reliable (independent monitoring, a longer record, a control site, North Quay data) is a strong finish.

Put the biggest weaknesses first, anchor every point in a detail from the source, and include at least one strength: an evaluation with no strengths reads as a list of complaints.

## 4. A real scheme to compare with: Medmerry

These figures are from memory and as of my training, so treat them as approximate and check them against your textbook or class notes before quoting. If your class used a different scheme, use that one instead.

- **Where:** Medmerry, on the Manhood Peninsula between Selsey and Bracklesham in West Sussex, on the open English Channel coast. Built by the Environment Agency; the RSPB now runs the site as a nature reserve. Confident.
- **When:** built 2011 to 2013, with the shingle bank breached in September 2013. Confident of 2013; fairly confident of the month.
- **Cost:** about £28 million. Fairly confident; this is the figure usually quoted.
- **Area:** about 183 ha of new intertidal habitat (saltmarsh and mudflat) within a site of roughly 300 ha. Fairly confident of the 183 ha; less sure of the total.
- **New defences:** about 7 km of new clay embankment set back inland. Fairly confident.
- **What it protects:** about 350 homes (348 is the figure I remember), the main road to Selsey and a water treatment works, raising the standard of protection from roughly once-a-year flooding to a much higher one. Confident of the scale; not sure of the exact return period.
- **Before the scheme:** the shingle bank was re-profiled by bulldozer every year, at a recurring cost I remember as a few hundred thousand pounds a year, but I am not confident of that figure.
- At the time it was described as the largest open-coast managed realignment in the UK. Fairly confident.

Comparison angles for Port Harrow:
- **Cost per hectare.** Port Harrow: £6,200,000 ÷ 95 ha = £65,263 per ha, about £65,000 per ha. Medmerry: £28,000,000 ÷ 183 ha = £153,005 per ha, about £153,000 per ha, with the uncertainty above. Medmerry was bigger, dearer and needed a long new embankment; Port Harrow is cheaper and says nothing about a new defence line.
- **Setting.** Open Channel coast with storm waves versus a sheltered estuary, so the wave climate, and therefore what "better" means, differs.
- **Purpose.** Medmerry had an explicit habitat-creation aim alongside flood protection; Source A gives only cost and flooding.
- **Evidence quality.** Medmerry was planned and monitored by a public agency; Source A was monitored by the beneficiary.

If your teacher prefers an estuary example, Alkborough Flats on the Humber estuary (breached in 2006, about 440 ha, roughly £10 million) is closer in setting, but I am less sure of its cost figure.
