# Haiti 2010 versus Chile 2010: why the smaller earthquake killed far more people

**Short answer.** Magnitude measures the energy released at the source. Deaths depend on how violently *populated* ground shook, and on how vulnerable and how well prepared those people were. Haiti's magnitude 7.0 was shallow, on land and about 25 km from a capital of roughly 2.5 to 3 million people living mostly in unreinforced concrete, in one of the poorest and least prepared states in the world. Chile's magnitude 8.8 was deeper, offshore and about 100 km from its nearest large city, in a wealthy country with strictly enforced seismic building codes and long earthquake experience. A large share of Chile's deaths came from the tsunami, which is exactly where its one serious failure (a missed warning) lay.

Many courses compress this into: risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability, reduced by capacity to cope. Each point below is written in the shape an "explain" answer needs: claim, mechanism, located evidence, link back to deaths.

## Five developed points

### 1. The hazard at the source: magnitude, depth and type of fault

- **Haiti, 12 January 2010, 16:53 local time (sure):** moment magnitude 7.0 (sure), focal depth about 13 km (sure), epicentre about 25 km west-south-west of Port-au-Prince, near the town of Léogâne (sure). It occurred in the boundary zone between the North American and Caribbean plates, which slide past each other (a conservative, or transform, boundary) at roughly 2 cm a year overall, with under 1 cm a year taken up on the Enriquillo–Plantain Garden fault along Haiti's southern peninsula (fairly sure). That left-lateral strike-slip fault had produced no major earthquake since 1751 and 1770 (sure), so around 250 years of strain had built up.
- **Chile, 27 February 2010, 03:34 local time (sure):** moment magnitude 8.8 (sure), among the six largest earthquakes recorded by instruments at that time (fairly sure), depth about 35 km (fairly sure; Chilean sources gave about 30 km), epicentre under the sea floor just off the Maule coast, roughly 100 km north-north-east of Concepción and about 325 km south-west of Santiago (fairly sure). It was a megathrust earthquake on the convergent (destructive) boundary where the oceanic Nazca plate subducts beneath the South American plate at around 7 to 8 cm a year (fairly sure). The rupture ran roughly 500 km along the plate interface (fairly sure), filling a known seismic gap unbroken since the 1835 Concepción earthquake that Charles Darwin described (fairly sure).
- **Why this matters for deaths:** subduction megathrusts have enormous fault areas, so they produce the largest magnitudes, but they lie offshore and tens of kilometres down, so the most violent shaking is spread along a long strip of coast and sea. A shallow strike-slip rupture under a city is smaller but concentrated: Haiti's rupture was only a few tens of kilometres long (fairly sure), and its energy arrived within seconds at dense settlements. Chile's shaking lasted roughly two to three minutes (fairly sure), which strains buildings badly, but Chilean buildings were designed for exactly that.

### 2. Where people were relative to the shaking (exposure and intensity)

- Magnitude is one number per earthquake; intensity (the Modified Mercalli scale) describes shaking at a place, and that is what kills. Intensity falls with distance and rises with shallow depth and soft or steep ground.
- **Haiti:** Léogâne and parts of Port-au-Prince reached intensity IX to X, the violent-to-extreme end of the scale (fairly sure). Léogâne, nearest the epicentre, had an estimated 80 to 90 per cent of buildings destroyed (fairly sure, UN assessment). The Port-au-Prince metropolitan area held around 2.5 to 3 million people (fairly sure), swollen by decades of rural-to-urban migration into unplanned hillside districts such as Carrefour and Delmas. Seismologists at the US Geological Survey (Hough and colleagues, 2010) found that ridges such as the one under Pétion-Ville amplified the shaking and concentrated damage (fairly sure). Late afternoon timing meant schools, offices and homes were all occupied (sure).
- **Chile:** the strongest shaking, roughly intensity VIII to IX (fairly sure), hit the Maule and Biobío coast, where towns are small. Concepción, the largest nearby city with a metropolitan population under 1 million (fairly sure), was about 100 km away; Santiago, with around 6 million people (fairly sure), was about 325 km away and felt roughly intensity VII to VIII (fairly sure). At 3:34 am most people were asleep at home, which in Chile meant inside engineered buildings (sure). The exception, important in point 5, was the many late-summer holidaymakers camping on the coast (fairly sure).
- **Link:** people exposed to intensity X in unreinforced buildings die in far greater numbers than people exposed to VII to VIII in engineered ones. Haiti's hazard was smaller in energy but far larger where it counts: at ground level under a capital city.

### 3. Wealth and building quality (vulnerability)

- **Haiti** was the poorest country in the Americas: GDP per person about US$650 to 700 in 2009 (fairly sure, World Bank-type figure), ranked 145th of 169 countries on the 2010 Human Development Index (fairly sure), with most of the population living on under about US$2 a day (fairly sure of the order; check the exact figure). There was no enforced national seismic building code (sure). Homes and public buildings alike were typically unreinforced concrete-block walls under heavy concrete slab roofs, often with extra storeys added informally, built with weak cement and untrained labour (sure). Under strong shaking they suffered pancake collapse, floor onto floor, which is why so many deaths were instant. The National Palace, the Parliament, the cathedral and the UN mission headquarters collapsed or were wrecked, and the UN lost 102 staff in Haiti, its worst single loss of life (sure). Around 1.5 million people were living in displacement camps by mid-2010 (fairly sure, International Organization for Migration figure).
- **Chile** was an upper-income country that joined the OECD in 2010 (fairly sure): GDP per person about US$10,000 in 2009 (fairly sure), ranked 45th of 169 on the 2010 Human Development Index (fairly sure). After the magnitude 9.5 Valdivia earthquake of 22 May 1960, the largest ever recorded (sure), and the magnitude 7.8 to 8.0 earthquake near Valparaíso and Santiago on 3 March 1985 (sure), Chile enforced a national seismic design standard (known as NCh433, revised in 1996; fairly sure of the name and date) requiring reinforced-concrete shear walls and engineer sign-off, with developers legally liable for structural failure for years after completion (fairly sure). Most buildings therefore stood. Failures were rare enough to become scandals: the 15-storey Alto Río apartment block in Concepción toppled (sure), and damage concentrated in older adobe housing in rural Maule (fairly sure).
- **Link:** wealth buys engineering, enforcement and spare capacity. Haiti's post-disaster needs assessment put damage and losses at about US$7.8 billion, roughly 120 per cent of its 2009 GDP (fairly sure); Chile's government estimated about US$30 billion, around 17 to 18 per cent of GDP (fairly sure). Chile lost about four times as much money and absorbed it; Haiti lost more than a year's output and could not.

### 4. Governance and preparedness before the event (vulnerability and capacity)

- **Haiti:** a weak, unstable state. The president had been forced from office in 2004 and a UN stabilisation mission had been in the country ever since (sure); Haiti sat near the bottom of Transparency International's corruption perceptions ranking around 2009 (fairly sure of the position, not the exact rank). Geologists had warned publicly in 2008 that the Enriquillo fault could produce an earthquake of around magnitude 7 (fairly sure), but there was no public education, no drills, no control of building on steep hillsides and no search-and-rescue capacity. The earthquake then destroyed the state's own capacity: most ministry buildings were destroyed or badly damaged (widely reported as 28 of 29; check), along with the main hospitals, the port and much police infrastructure (fairly sure).
- **Chile:** a stable, well-funded state with a national emergency office (ONEMI), earthquake drills in schools, a population that had lived through 1960 and 1985, and hospitals and fire services built to keep working (fairly sure). The government declared a state of catastrophe and deployed the army within a day or two (fairly sure), even with a presidential handover due on 11 March 2010 (fairly sure). It was criticised for slowness, and there was looting in Concepción before troops arrived (fairly sure), but the basic machinery worked.
- **Link:** preparedness turns a hazard into an emergency; its absence turns it into a catastrophe. A disaster is a social outcome, not only a physical event.

### 5. After the shaking: response capacity and secondary hazards

- **Haiti:** with hospitals wrecked, a single damaged airport, a wrecked port and no heavy lifting equipment, most survivors were dug out by neighbours by hand (sure). The government reported around 300,000 injured (fairly sure); many of them died in the following days for want of surgery, which is one reason the death toll kept rising and is so hard to count. A small local tsunami also killed a handful of people near Léogâne (fairly sure).
- **Chile:** the shaking itself killed relatively few. The subduction rupture lifted the sea floor and sent a tsunami several metres high, locally much higher, into Constitución, Dichato, Talcahuano and Robinson Crusoe Island in the Juan Fernández archipelago (fairly sure). The navy's hydrographic service and ONEMI failed to issue a clear, timely warning, and the president initially told the public there was no tsunami risk (fairly sure); officials later faced criminal charges over this (fairly sure). Roughly a quarter to a third of Chile's deaths, including holidaymakers camping on beaches, are attributed to the tsunami (fairly sure of the order, not the exact number).
- **Link:** even in Chile, the deaths that did occur clustered where the system failed. That confirms the argument: vulnerability and capacity, not magnitude, explain the gap.

## Energy: how many times more than the magnitude 7.0?

About 500 times. Neither classmate is right, and here is why.

The moment magnitude scale is logarithmic. The moment-magnitude definition (Hanks and Kanamori, 1979) and the older Gutenberg–Richter energy relation both make energy rise with 1.5 × magnitude, and the usual textbook form is:

log₁₀ (energy in joules) ≈ 1.5 × M + 4.8

So each whole magnitude step multiplies energy by 10^1.5 ≈ 31.6 (the "about 32 times" rule), and the energy ratio between two earthquakes is 10 raised to the power (1.5 × magnitude difference).

Working:
1. Magnitude difference: 8.8 − 7.0 = 1.8
2. Exponent: 1.5 × 1.8 = 2.7
3. Energy ratio = 10^2.7 = 10^2 × 10^0.7
4. 10^0.7 ≈ 5.01 (because log₁₀ 5 ≈ 0.699)
5. Ratio ≈ 100 × 5.01 ≈ 501, so about 500 times (rounded to one significant figure)

Check by working out each energy:
- M 7.0: log₁₀ E = 1.5 × 7.0 + 4.8 = 10.5 + 4.8 = 15.3, so E ≈ 10^0.3 × 10^15 ≈ 2.0 × 10^15 J
- M 8.8: log₁₀ E = 1.5 × 8.8 + 4.8 = 13.2 + 4.8 = 18.0, so E = 1.0 × 10^18 J
- Ratio = 1.0 × 10^18 ÷ (2.0 × 10^15) ≈ 500, which agrees

Where the classmates went wrong:
- "about 18 times because it's 1.8 higher" treats a logarithmic scale as linear. A magnitude difference is an exponent, not a multiplier (and 1.8 is not 18 in any case).
- "10 times per unit so about 60 times" uses the right rule for the wrong quantity. The amplitude of ground motion on a seismogram rises about 10 times per magnitude unit, so the amplitude ratio is 10^1.8 = 10 × 10^0.8 ≈ 10 × 6.3 ≈ 63. Energy rises about 32 times per unit, which is where 500 comes from.

So the Chile earthquake released about 500 times more energy, yet on any estimate between roughly 90 and 600 times fewer people died. That is the hook for your whole answer: magnitude did not decide the death toll.
