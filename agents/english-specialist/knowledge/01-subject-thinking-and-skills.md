# English specialist: subject thinking and skills

This is background for the tutor, not text to show students. It sits under your
master prompt and your own knowledge base. General answering rules shared by every
specialist are in the shared answering document.

**Scope note.** Your exact scope is set by your master prompt, which this document
has not seen. It is probably an English A course, most likely English A:
Literature, but that is unverified. The skills below apply across the English
courses. Section 1 helps you tell the courses apart so that you give course-specific
advice only for the course your master prompt covers and the student actually takes.

## 1. Telling the English courses apart

Students often just say "English". Before giving advice that depends on the course
(exam tasks, oral format, coursework), work out which one they take. The signals
below are heuristics, and course details change between guide editions, so verify
each against the current IB subject guide before stating it as fact.

| Course | What it studies | Signals in a student's message |
| --- | --- | --- |
| English A: Literature | Literary works only: poetry, prose, drama, literary non-fiction, including works in translation | Only novels, plays and poems mentioned; "works in translation"; an unseen poem or prose passage |
| English A: Language and Literature | Literary works and non-literary texts (adverts, speeches, articles, images, posts) | "Text types", adverts, cartoons, infographics, "body of work", language and power |
| English B (language acquisition) | Learning English as an additional language: reading, listening, writing and speaking for real purposes | "Text types" for *writing* (email, blog, article); themes; listening practice; English is not the student's strongest language |

Overlaps to remember:
- Close reading of a literary work is common to both English A courses.
- "Text type" appears in Language and Literature (for analysis) and in English B
  (mainly for writing), so on its own it does not settle the question.
- "Paper 1" and "Paper 2" exist in several courses but mean different tasks, so the
  paper name alone does not identify the course.

When the course matters and is unclear, ask **one** question ("Are you taking English
A: Literature, English A: Language and Literature, or English B?"). When it does not
matter, for example a question about embedding quotations, answer and say the advice
applies across courses.

## 2. Close reading: a routine for any text

1. **Literal first.** Who is speaking, to whom, about what, where, when?
2. **Notice.** Mark what stands out: repetition, shifts, contrasts, unusual words,
   gaps, a change of pace or register.
3. **Pattern.** Group observations into two or three patterns. A pattern is more
   persuasive than a single device.
4. **Effect.** For each pattern, say precisely what it does: to tone, pace, sympathy,
   the reader's position.
5. **Meaning.** Connect the effects to an idea the text explores or a position it
   takes.
6. **Qualify.** Is there another reading? What would complicate yours?

## 3. Quotation integrity

This is a habit for students and a rule for the tutor.

**For the tutor:** quote only text present in the conversation or lines in your
knowledge documents. Never quote from memory, even famous lines. If a student's
quotation cannot be checked, say so.

**For students, how to quote well:**
- **Exact words.** Copy spelling, capitals and punctuation exactly from your
  edition. Editions and translations differ, so use the one you study.
- **Short and embedded.** Build quotations into your own sentence: The narrator calls
  the house "a quiet animal", which… rather than a long block followed by
  "This quote shows…".
- **Mark omissions** with an ellipsis in square brackets, "[…]". Never join two
  separate phrases as if they were one.
- **Square brackets for changes**, for example to fit grammar: "[she] waited".
- **Poetry line breaks** are marked " / " and stanza breaks " // ".
- **Paraphrase honestly.** If you are not sure of the exact words, paraphrase and do
  not use quotation marks.

## 4. Analytical paragraph craft

A flexible pattern, not a formula:

1. **Topic sentence that argues**, not one that announces a device.
   Weak: "The writer uses imagery." Better: "The garden imagery turns the
   narrator's grief into something that keeps growing."
2. **Evidence**: one or two short, embedded quotations.
3. **Analysis**: zoom in on a word or choice and explain its effect; zoom out to its
   significance.
4. **Development**: a second piece of evidence, an alternative reading, or a
   complication.
5. **Link**: connect to the thesis without just repeating it.

**Illustration (invented passage, written for this document):**

> She kept the letter in the bread tin, under the loaf, where nobody would think to
> look and where she could touch it every morning.

A strong analytical sentence: "Hiding the letter 'under the loaf' places it inside
the most ordinary daily routine, so the secret is both concealed and constantly
touched; the sentence ends on 'every morning', suggesting she does not want to
forget it as much as she wants nobody else to find it."

## 5. Essay craft

- **Thesis.** An arguable answer to the actual question, specific enough to be
  wrong. It can carry a "because" or a "whereas".
- **Introduction.** Brief: the question's key terms, the texts, the thesis. No
  dictionary definitions or sweeping claims about "all of human history".
- **Body.** Each paragraph advances the argument; the order builds (for example,
  from obvious to subtle, or from one text's method to the other's).
- **Counter-reading.** Strong essays consider another interpretation and explain
  why theirs fits more of the evidence.
- **Conclusion.** Says what the argument adds up to; may widen to significance. It is
  not a summary of each paragraph.
- **Register.** Formal, precise, present tense for describing what a text does.

## 6. Comparative analysis

- Compare through a shared question, not "text A, then text B".
- Compare **methods**, not only themes: how each writer presents the idea.
- Use comparative connectives that carry meaning: "whereas", "by contrast", "both…
  but", "similarly, though more…".
- Differences are usually more revealing than similarities.

## 7. Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
| --- | --- |
| "Naming a device is analysis." | Name, then explain effect and meaning. |
| "Long quotations show I know the text." | Short, exact, embedded quotations analysed closely do more. |
| "My interpretation must match the teacher's." | Interpretations are judged on support and coherence. |
| "The speaker is the writer." | Say "the speaker" or "the narrator". |
| "All English courses want the same thing." | Core skills overlap; assessment tasks differ by course. Check which course before giving task-specific advice. |
| "Context goes in its own paragraph." | Use context only where it sharpens a specific reading. |
