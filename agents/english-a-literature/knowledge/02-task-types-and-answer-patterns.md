# English A: Literature: task types and answer patterns

Students bring a small number of recurring task types. They are described here in
general terms. Students often use names such as "Paper 1", "Paper 2", "the IO" or
"the HL essay"; those names and the formats behind them can change between guide
editions (verify against the current IB subject guide). Do not state durations,
weightings, word limits or mark totals unless your master prompt or knowledge base
gives them. If the student gives you a word limit or rubric, work with theirs.

## 1. Unseen or guided analysis of a literary passage

**What students bring:** a poem or prose extract they have not studied, often with a
guiding question; requests for an approach; practice passages; their own timed
attempts.

**Approach to teach:**
1. Read the whole passage twice. Settle the literal sense first: who speaks, what
   happens, where and when.
2. Use any guiding question as a lens, not a cage. It points to something
   significant but does not limit what the student may discuss (verify how guiding
   questions are framed against the current IB subject guide).
3. Annotate for three or four significant patterns, not every device.
4. Write a thesis about *how* the passage creates its meaning or effect.
5. Organise sections by idea or by the passage's movement, not by device ("the
   imagery paragraph", "the sound paragraph").
6. In each paragraph: claim, embedded evidence, close analysis, link back to the
   thesis.
7. End with what the whole passage adds up to, not a summary.

**Strong answers contain:** a precise, arguable thesis; attention to the whole
passage, especially the ending; structure and form as well as language; short,
exact, embedded quotations; terminology used for a purpose; interpretation that is
tentative where the evidence is open.

**Tutor's role:** practise with the student on a passage they paste. When modelling
a full response, prefer a different passage (invented, or public domain) so the
student does the thinking on their own text. A full model on the student's own
passage is allowed only for openly ungraded practice.

## 2. Comparative essay on two works

**What students bring:** choosing works for a question; structuring a comparison;
revising quotations; timed practice (verify the format, including whether students
have the texts with them in the exam, against the current IB subject guide).

**Approach to teach:**
- Unpack the question's key terms and decide what is genuinely being asked.
- Choose works that give plenty to say on *both* sides of the question.
- Build a comparative thesis: "Both A and B …, but whereas A …, B …"
- Prefer an integrated structure. Each paragraph should compare a method, not
  describe one text.
- Keep the focus on how writers present ideas, not only on what the ideas are.

**Quotations from memory.** Students revise short, exact quotations. Help them
choose which moments to learn from their own copy of the text. Do not supply
quotations from memory: if a student asks for "five quotes about guilt", point to
the relevant moments and ask them to paste or copy the exact words from their
edition.

## 3. Oral preparation

**What students bring:** choosing extracts, finding a global issue that links them,
structuring a talk, rehearsing answers to follow-up questions (verify the format,
including number of works, extract length, timing and the role of a global issue,
against the current IB subject guide).

**Approach to teach:**
- The issue must be specific and genuinely present in both texts. "Power" is a
  topic; "how people with little social power use silence as resistance" is a
  focus.
- Balance the texts. Analyse authorial choices in each extract, not just content.
- A clear route: the issue, extract one, extract two, the connection between them,
  a conclusion that says something new.
- Rehearse aloud. Talking from brief notes usually sounds more convincing than
  reciting a script (verify what notes are permitted against the current IB subject
  guide).

**Tutor's role:** ask the probing questions a listener would ask; give feedback on
outlines and spoken drafts the student types up; play the role of the listener. Do
not write a script for the student to memorise.

## 4. Longer coursework essay (HL)

**What students bring:** finding a line of inquiry, narrowing it, structuring and
revising drafts (verify the requirements and word limit against the current IB
subject guide).

**Approach to teach:** move from a broad interest ("I liked the ending") to a
focused, arguable question ("How does the ending's shift in narrative voice change
our judgement of the narrator?"). Check that the text offers enough evidence.
Outline, draft, then revise for argument before polishing sentences.

**Tutor's role:** help choose and narrow the focus with questions, comment on
outlines and drafts, mark provisionally if the student supplies the criteria. Never
write any part of the essay.

## 5. Reading notes and learner portfolio

Low-stakes reflective work. Suggest entry types that build analytical habits: a
close reading of a single moment, a question log, a comparison of two scenes, a map
of a motif, a short creative response followed by a reflection. Help the student
think; do not write entries for them.

## 6. Command terms and depth

The command term tells you how far to go. In literature questions, "how" is
analytical: it asks about methods and effects, not a description of content.

| Term | What the answer should do |
| --- | --- |
| Identify, state | Give the feature or fact briefly. No essay. |
| Outline, describe | Give an account of what is there, with little interpretation. |
| Explain | Say why or how, with reasons linked to the text. |
| Analyse | Break the passage into choices and show what each contributes. |
| Compare, compare and contrast | Hold both texts together; show likeness and difference in method and effect. |
| Examine, discuss | Explore several angles or readings with evidence. |
| Evaluate, to what extent | Reach a judgement weighed against a counter-reading. |

If a question is open ("Write about the presentation of power"), treat it as
analytical and say so.

## 7. Giving provisional criterion-based feedback

### Procedure
1. **Establish the task and criteria.** Use the official criteria if your master
   prompt or knowledge base contains them, or the rubric the student pastes. If you
   have neither, ask which task it is, or give qualitative feedback without a
   number.
2. **Read the whole piece first.** Comment on the argument before sentence-level
   detail.
3. **Lead with a one- or two-sentence verdict.**
4. **Two or three strengths**, each pointing to the student's own words, quoted
   exactly.
5. **Two or three priorities**, ordered by impact, each with a concrete action ("Cut
   the plot summary in sentences 2 and 3; replace it with one sentence on why the
   narrator withholds the name").
6. **Provisional mark only if the criteria are known**, labelled in the same
   sentence: "Provisional mark: 3/5 on the analysis scale you gave me; only your
   teacher and the IB award real marks."
7. **One next step** the student can do now.

**Do not** rewrite the student's paragraph, hand back a polished version, or inflate
praise. You may show how a *different* sentence on a *different* example could be
improved.

### Illustrative rubric (illustrative, not the official IB criteria)

Use this only for qualitative feedback when no official or teacher criteria are
available. Report a level as "Provisional level: secure (on an illustrative scale,
not the official IB criteria)".

| Dimension | Developing | Secure | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Understanding and interpretation | Mostly literal; some misreading | Sound reading with a clear interpretation | Perceptive, nuanced interpretation that weighs alternatives |
| Analysis of the writer's choices | Names features or retells | Explains effects of well-chosen features | Links choices to meaning across the whole text, precisely |
| Organisation and argument | Ideas listed; thesis unclear | Clear thesis; paragraphs support it | Argument develops; each section builds on the last |
| Expression | Unclear in places; terms misused | Clear, accurate, appropriate register | Precise, economical, purposeful use of terminology |

## 8. Checklist: what strong answers usually contain

- An arguable thesis that answers the actual question.
- Analysis of method (form, structure, language), not only content.
- Short, exact, embedded quotations from the student's own copy.
- Context used only where it sharpens a reading.
- At least one alternative reading considered.
- A conclusion that says what the analysis adds up to.
