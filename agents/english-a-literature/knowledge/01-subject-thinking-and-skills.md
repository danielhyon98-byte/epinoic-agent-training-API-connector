# English A: Literature: subject thinking and skills

This is background for the tutor, not text to show students. It sits under your
master prompt and your own knowledge base. General answering rules shared by every
specialist are in the shared answering document; this file covers the subject.

## 1. What the course asks students to do

English A: Literature is for students who read and write English at a high level.
They study **literary works**: poetry, prose fiction, drama and literary
non-fiction, from different periods and places. Some works are read in translation
(verify the current reading-list requirements against the current IB subject guide).

Across the course, students learn to:

- read closely and notice how a writer's choices shape meaning and effect;
- build interpretations and defend them with evidence from the text;
- consider how the contexts in which a work was written and is read change its
  meaning;
- compare works and connect them with wider issues and ideas;
- write and speak about literature clearly, with organised, well-evidenced argument.

The course is framed by a few broad lenses: roughly, how readers, writers and texts
make meaning together; how time and place shape texts; and how texts connect with
other texts. A set of recurring concepts, such as identity, perspective and
representation, runs alongside them (verify the current names of these areas and
concepts against the current IB subject guide). Treat them as ways of asking
questions, not as boxes to tick.

**How this differs from English A: Language and Literature.** This course works
with literary works. Language and Literature also studies non-literary texts, such
as adverts, speeches, articles and images, and the ways language works in society.
If a student brings an advert or a news article for analysis, check which course
they take before going further (see the answering-functionality document).

## 2. The core move: choice, effect, meaning

Almost every strong piece of literary analysis repeats one move:

1. **Notice a choice** the writer made: a word, an image, a line break, a shift in
   narrative voice, a stage direction, a silence.
2. **Describe its effect** precisely: on tone, on pace, on the reader's sympathy, on
   how a character appears.
3. **Connect it to meaning**: what it contributes to the work's larger ideas,
   tensions or questions.

A technical term is only useful when it leads to steps 2 and 3. "The writer uses
personification" is an observation. It becomes analysis when the student says what
the device does here and why that matters.

**Illustration (invented passage, written for this document):**

> The kettle shrieked. Nobody moved. In the next room the clock kept counting,
> politely, as if nothing had been said.

- *Weaker:* "The writer uses personification of the clock to create tension."
- *Stronger:* "Two clipped sentences ('The kettle shrieked. Nobody moved.') freeze
  the scene, and then the longer final sentence lets the clock carry on 'politely'.
  The objects hold the emotion the people will not show: the kettle screams, the
  clock keeps up appearances, and the characters' silence becomes the real event."

The stronger version names the choices, describes their effect and arrives at an
interpretation that could be argued with.

## 3. Reading by form

### Poetry
- **Speaker and voice.** Say "the speaker", not the poet's name, unless the
  biographical link is the point and is supported.
- **Lines and breaks.** Enjambment carries sense across a line break and can create
  momentum, suspense or a double meaning. End-stopping closes a thought. A caesura (a
  strong pause inside a line) can fracture rhythm.
- **Sound.** Rhyme, metre, rhythm, alliteration, assonance, sibilance. Always link
  the sound to the sense; never list sound devices on their own.
- **Imagery and figurative language.** Simile, metaphor, personification, symbol.
  Look for patterns of images across the poem, not single examples.
- **Structure.** Stanza shape, the turn (where tone or argument shifts), repetition,
  refrain, and how the ending reframes the opening.
- **Form as meaning.** Readers bring expectations to a sonnet, an elegy, a dramatic
  monologue or free verse. Writers use those expectations, and break them.

*Public-domain example.* Wordsworth begins "I wandered lonely as a cloud / That
floats on high o'er vales and hills," and the first line has no punctuation at its
end, so the sentence drifts over the line break much as the cloud drifts. (Wording
as in common modern editions; check the student's edition.)

### Prose fiction
- **Narrative voice.** First or third person; reliable or not; whose perceptions
  filter the scene (focalisation); free indirect style, where narration takes on a
  character's thoughts and idiom without quotation marks.
- **Time.** Order, pace, flashback, gaps, summary versus fully dramatised scene.
- **Character.** Direct description, action, dialogue, and what characters avoid
  saying.
- **Setting, symbol and motif.** How place and recurring objects carry meaning.
- **Style.** Sentence length and shape, diction, register, rhythm.
- **Openings and endings.** What the first lines promise and the last lines settle
  or unsettle.

*Public-domain example.* Austen opens *Pride and Prejudice* with "It is a truth
universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must
be in want of a wife." The grand, philosophical phrasing ("a truth universally
acknowledged") is applied to village marriage-hunting, and the logic is reversed:
it is the neighbours who want the rich man, not the man who wants a wife. The irony
sets the narrator's tone for the whole novel.

### Drama
- Plays are written to be performed. Think about bodies, space, sound, lighting,
  silence and what an audience sees.
- Stage directions, entrances and exits.
- Dialogue as action: characters persuade, threaten, conceal. Look for subtext.
- Dramatic irony, soliloquy and aside: who knows what, and when.
- Structure: scenes, turning points, the placing of a climax.

*Public-domain example.* In *Macbeth* the line "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and
tomorrow," uses repetition and a heavy, even rhythm to make time itself sound
monotonous, just after Macbeth hears that the Queen is dead. (Spelling and
punctuation vary between editions.)

### Literary non-fiction
Memoir, essays, letters and travel writing construct a persona, select and order
experience, and use rhetoric. Analyse the constructed "I" as carefully as a
fictional narrator.

## 4. Context: use it, do not dump it

- **Context of production:** when, where, by whom and for whom the work was
  written. **Context of reception:** how different readers, then and now, respond.
- Context earns its place when it changes how we read a *specific detail*. A
  paragraph of biography at the start of an essay usually does not.
- Do not treat a writer's life as proof of what a line means. "Owen served as a
  soldier in the First World War" is a fact; "therefore this image shows his own
  trauma" is an interpretation that needs the text's support.
- **Works in translation.** The exact words on the page were chosen by a translator.
  When analysis depends on sound or a single word, acknowledge this ("in this
  translation") and do not attribute the word choice to the original author without
  qualification.

## 5. Interpretation: arguable, supported, qualified

- A **thesis** is arguable: a thoughtful reader could disagree with it.
  "The poem is about loss" is a topic. "The poem presents loss as something the
  speaker half-welcomes, because grief keeps the dead close" is a thesis.
- Use **tentative language** for interpretation ("suggests", "may imply", "invites
  the reader to") and plain statement for what is observably in the text.
- Keep **fact, interpretation and judgement** apart. "The poem has four stanzas" is
  a fact. "The fourth stanza turns bitter" is an interpretation. "This makes the
  ending more powerful than the opening" is a judgement.
- Strong work considers an **alternative reading** and explains why its own reading
  fits more of the evidence.

## 6. Comparison

- Compare through a shared question, not by writing about one text and then the
  other with a single linking sentence.
- Compare **methods** (how each writer presents an idea), not only themes.
- Differences are often more interesting than similarities. "Both texts present
  grief" is a starting point, not a conclusion.
- An integrated structure (each paragraph addresses both works) usually keeps the
  comparison alive. A block structure can work if it is tightly linked.

## 7. Common misconceptions and how to correct them

| Misconception | How to correct it |
| --- | --- |
| "Spotting techniques is analysis." | Ask "What does it do here, and why does that matter?" Model the choice, effect, meaning move once, then let the student try. |
| "There is one right interpretation the teacher wants." | Interpretations are judged on support and coherence. Show two defensible readings of the same detail. |
| "The author meant X." | Shift the focus to what the text does. Intent is usually unknowable; effect is arguable from the text. |
| "Context goes first, then analysis." | Integrate context only where it sharpens the reading of a particular moment. |
| "More quotations means a better essay." | Fewer, shorter, embedded quotations, each analysed closely, are worth more. |
| "Retelling the plot shows understanding." | Plot is only for locating a moment. Ask "So what?" after every summary sentence. |
| "The speaker or narrator is the writer." | Use "the speaker" or "the narrator". The writer constructs that voice. |
| "Every detail is deeply symbolic." | Keep claims in proportion to the evidence. A comma is not always a cry for help. |
| "Every word in a translated work is the author's choice." | Acknowledge the translator, especially for word-level and sound analysis. |
| "Personal response means saying I liked it." | A personal response is valuable when it is precise and grounded in the text: what in the text produced that reaction, and why. |

**Correcting well.** Name the habit kindly, show one improved version on a
different example (invented, or a public-domain line you are sure of), then ask the
student to revise their own sentence. Do not rewrite their work for them.
