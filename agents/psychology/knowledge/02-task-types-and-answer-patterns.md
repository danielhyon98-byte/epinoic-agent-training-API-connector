# Psychology: task types and answer patterns

Recognise the task type before you answer. Paper names, question formats, mark
totals, timings and weightings are deliberately not given here, partly because the
course has been revised. If a student asks about them, use your own knowledge base,
or tell them to check the current IB subject guide or their teacher.

## 1. Definitions

A precise sentence in the student's own words: term → what it is → the qualifier that
separates it from similar terms.

- Weak: "Neuroplasticity is when the brain changes."
- Strong: "Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change its structure and
  connections in response to experience, learning or injury."

## 2. Short answers on a theory, concept or study

Typical command terms are "Describe", "Outline" and "Explain".

**Pattern:**

1. Define the key term in the question.
2. Present one relevant study in the checkable order: researcher(s) and year (only
   if confident), aim, method, participants, brief procedure, findings.
3. **Explain the link**: how the findings support the concept. This is the step
   students most often skip.
4. One closing sentence that answers the question.

Do not evaluate unless the command term asks for it.

## 3. Extended responses ("Evaluate", "Discuss", "To what extent", "Contrast")

**Pattern of a strong answer:**

1. **Introduction**: define key terms, name the theory or theories and studies you
   will use, and state your line of argument.
2. **Body**: usually two or three pieces of research, each described accurately and
   linked explicitly to the question.
3. **Critical thinking** throughout, not saved for the end: methodological strengths
   and limits, ethics, cultural and gender considerations, alternative explanations,
   contradictory evidence, and how far findings generalise.
4. **Conclusion**: a judgement that answers the exact question and says what it
   depends on.

"Contrast" questions need point-by-point differences, not two separate descriptions.
"To what extent" needs a stated extent ("largely", "only partly") with reasons.

## 4. Research-methods questions and unseen studies

Students may be given a short description of a study and asked about its method,
sampling, ethics, credibility or generalisability. How the student's course version
assesses this, and at which level, should be checked (verify against the current IB subject guide).

- Work **only** from the text the student pastes. Never fill gaps with invented
  details about the study.
- Name the feature, then show where the text reveals it ("The researcher recruited
  through a parents' group, so this is purposive or possibly snowball sampling").
- For qualitative studies, use qualitative concepts (credibility, transferability,
  triangulation, reflexivity), not only experimental ones.
- If the text is missing, explain the method of answering and ask for the text.

## 5. Statistics and data questions

- Show working for descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range, standard
  deviation).
- Describe graphs in words: type of graph, axes and units, what each bar or point
  represents, and the pattern.
- Explain test choice by design (independent or repeated) and data level.
- Interpret significance carefully: "unlikely to be due to chance alone", not
  "proves".
- Distinguish percentages from percentage points when comparing conditions ("60% of
  condition A recalled the word versus 45% of condition B, a difference of 15
  percentage points").

## 6. Internal assessment guidance

In the version of the course many students currently follow, the IA is an
experimental study in which students plan, carry out and report a simple experiment,
usually based on a published one. Check the requirements for the student's course
version and exam session, including word limits, group-work rules and ethical rules
(verify against the current IB subject guide).

What you may do:

- explain experimental design, operationalisation, hypotheses, controls and
  descriptive or inferential statistics using a *different* example;
- ask questions that help the student design their own procedure;
- explain the ethical requirements their teacher has given them, and why they matter;
- give feedback on their own draft, quoting their sentences.

What you must not do: write any section of the report, write their hypotheses for
their study, choose their materials, or analyse their data for them in submittable
form.

## 7. Feedback on drafts

1. Establish the task: exact question, command term, and the criteria or scheme
   their teacher uses.
2. Say briefly and specifically what works.
3. Give the two or three changes that would help most, each tied to a quoted phrase.
4. **Correct factual errors about studies first**: wrong researcher, year, method or
   finding. Overclaiming ("proves", "causes" from a correlation) comes next.
5. Label any mark provisional in the same sentence (see section 9).

## 8. Command terms and the depth they need

| Command term | What the answer must do |
| --- | --- |
| Define | Give the precise meaning. |
| Identify, state | Give the name or fact, no explanation. |
| Outline | Give a brief account. |
| Describe | Give a detailed account of what was done or found, without reasons. |
| Explain | Give reasons and show how the evidence supports the point. |
| Analyse | Break the issue into parts and show how they connect. |
| Compare, contrast | Give similarities or differences point by point. |
| Discuss | Give a balanced review of arguments and evidence, then a conclusion. |
| Evaluate | Weigh strengths and limitations, then judge. |
| Examine | Look at an argument or concept to bring out its assumptions and links. |
| To what extent | Judge how far a claim holds, with reasons and evidence. |
| Suggest | Propose a reasonable answer or explanation. |

These are descriptions in our own words. Use the official command-term wording in
your own knowledge base where it exists.

## 9. Provisional criterion-based feedback

Mark against the criteria that apply to the task. If you do not know them, ask once,
or give qualitative feedback with no number.

**Illustrative feedback rubric for a Psychology extended response (illustrative, not
the official IB criteria):**

| Strand | What strong work shows |
| --- | --- |
| Focus on the question | Every paragraph answers the question asked, using its key terms |
| Knowledge and understanding | Accurate theories and terms |
| Use of research | Correctly attributed studies, described accurately and linked to the point |
| Critical thinking | Methodological, ethical and cultural evaluation, and alternative explanations |
| Organisation and clarity | A clear line of argument and a conclusion that answers the question |

How to phrase a mark: put the provisional label in the **same sentence** as the
number. For example: "Provisional mark: 4 out of 6 against your teacher's scheme;
only your teacher and the IB award real marks." Never leave a bare number elsewhere
in the answer. If one missing piece (the exact question, the rubric) could change the
mark a lot, give no number or a provisional range.

## 10. Topics touching mental health

Abnormal psychology, stress, trauma, addiction and similar topics are taught as
**academic content**. Keep the framing educational: explain theories, studies and
evaluation; use respectful, person-first language ("a person with depression");
avoid sensational detail. Never diagnose the student or anyone they describe. The
answering document explains what to do if a student discloses personal distress.
