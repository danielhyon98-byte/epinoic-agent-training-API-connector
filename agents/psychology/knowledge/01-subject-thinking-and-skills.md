# Psychology: subject thinking and skills

This document adds to your own Psychology knowledge base. It does not replace it.
If anything here disagrees with your master prompt or knowledge base, follow those
and treat this text as wrong.

The IB Psychology course has been revised. Depending on their exam session, a
student may follow the older version (organised around biological, cognitive and
sociocultural approaches plus optional topics) or the newer one, which organises
content differently. Which structure applies to which exam session should be checked
(verify against the current IB subject guide). The thinking skills below apply to both.

## 1. How a psychologist thinks

- **Behaviour and mental processes are studied with evidence.** Claims rest on
  studies, and studies have methods with strengths and limits.
- **Theory, study, finding and interpretation are different things.** A *theory*
  (schema theory) explains. A *study* (Bartlett, 1932) tests or illustrates it. A
  *finding* is what was observed (participants changed unfamiliar details of a story
  when recalling it). An *interpretation* is what the researchers or we conclude
  (memory is reconstructed using existing schemas). Keep the four apart.
- **Levels of explanation interact.** Biological, cognitive and sociocultural
  factors work together. Strong answers avoid reducing behaviour to one cause.
- **Correlation is not causation.** Only well-controlled experiments allow strong
  causal claims, and even then within their conditions.
- **Measurement matters.** Constructs such as memory, stress or prejudice must be
  operationalised, and the choice of measure affects the conclusion.
- **Culture, gender and sample.** Much classic research used narrow samples, often
  Western university students. Ask who was studied before generalising.
- **Ethics runs through everything**: how research treats participants, and how
  findings are used.

## 2. Key areas students ask about

Your knowledge base holds the syllabus detail. Common areas:

- **Biological:** localisation of function, neuroplasticity, neurotransmission,
  hormones, genetics and behaviour (twin, family and adoption studies), evolutionary
  explanations, brain imaging techniques, animal research.
- **Cognitive:** models of memory (multi-store, working memory), schema theory,
  reconstructive memory, thinking and decision-making (heuristics and biases),
  emotion and memory, the reliability of cognitive processes.
- **Sociocultural:** social identity theory, social cognitive (observational)
  learning, stereotypes, conformity and obedience, culture and cultural norms,
  enculturation and acculturation.
- **Applied areas:** such as mental health, human development, health and
  well-being, and relationships. Which of these the student's course version
  includes should be checked (verify against the current IB subject guide).

## 3. Research methods

| Concept | What to make clear |
| --- | --- |
| Experiment types | Laboratory and field experiments manipulate an IV with random allocation. Natural experiments use an IV that varies naturally. Quasi-experiments compare pre-existing groups (for example age groups), so allocation is not random. |
| IV, DV, operationalisation | The IV is what changes, the DV is what is measured, and both must be defined precisely enough to repeat. |
| Hypotheses | A testable prediction, either directional or non-directional, with a null hypothesis of no effect or difference. |
| Designs | Independent samples, repeated measures (with counterbalancing for order effects), matched pairs. |
| Sampling | Random, opportunity (convenience), self-selected (volunteer), purposive, snowball, each with its own bias risk. |
| Validity | Internal (did the IV cause the change?), external and ecological (does it generalise?), construct (does the measure capture the concept?). |
| Reliability | Consistency of a measure or finding when repeated. |
| Threats | Confounding variables, demand characteristics, researcher bias, participant expectations. |
| Correlational studies | Show relationships, not causes; watch for third variables. |
| Qualitative methods | Interviews, observations, case studies and focus groups, judged by credibility, transferability, triangulation and reflexivity rather than statistical generalisation. |

### Descriptive statistics, worked (hypothetical data)

Recall scores for six participants: 4, 6, 7, 7, 9, 12.

- Mean = (4 + 6 + 7 + 7 + 9 + 12) ÷ 6 = 45 ÷ 6 = **7.5**
- Median = average of the 3rd and 4th values = (7 + 7) ÷ 2 = **7**
- Mode = **7**. Range = 12 − 4 = **8**
- Sample standard deviation (dividing by n − 1): squared deviations from 7.5 sum to
  37.5; 37.5 ÷ 5 = 7.5; √7.5 ≈ **2.74**

When comparing conditions, choose a test by design and data level, for example an
independent-samples test for two separate groups and a repeated-measures test for
the same participants, and parametric or non-parametric tests depending on the data.
A significant result (commonly p < 0.05) means the difference is unlikely to be due
to chance alone if the null hypothesis is true. It does not mean the effect is large
or important.

## 4. Ethics

- **Research with people:** informed consent (and parental consent for children),
  avoiding unjustified deception, debriefing, right to withdraw (including their
  data), protection from physical and psychological harm, confidentiality and
  anonymity.
- **Research with animals:** justification, minimising number and suffering, and
  the question of whether findings transfer to humans.
- **Applying findings:** labelling and stigma in diagnosis, consequences of genetic
  explanations, and who benefits from the research.

Teach ethics as **specific to the study**: not "it was unethical" but "participants
were not told the real aim, so their consent was not fully informed, although they
were debriefed afterwards".

## 5. Describing and evaluating a study

Use this order, which also makes the attribution checkable:

1. **Researcher(s) and year**, only if confident. Otherwise describe the study
   without a name ("a well-known study of London taxi drivers") and suggest the
   student check the details in their textbook.
2. **Aim.**
3. **Method and design** (lab experiment, correlational, case study…).
4. **Participants** (who, how many if known, how sampled).
5. **Procedure**, in brief.
6. **Findings**: what was observed.
7. **Conclusion or interpretation**: what it suggests, clearly marked as
   interpretation.
8. **Link** to the question.
9. **Evaluation**: validity, reliability, sample and generalisation, ethics,
   alternative explanations, supporting or contradicting evidence.

**Well-established examples** (name only these kinds of studies unless your own
knowledge base gives others; check fine details such as participant numbers before
stating them):

| Study | Method | Key finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bartlett (1932) | Serial and repeated reproduction of an unfamiliar folk tale ("The War of the Ghosts") | Participants shortened and altered the story to fit their own cultural expectations |
| Scoville and Milner (1957) | Case study of patient HM after surgery that removed parts of both medial temporal lobes | Severe difficulty forming new long-term memories |
| Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961) | Laboratory experiment with young children who watched an adult model | Children who saw an aggressive model imitated more aggression towards a Bobo doll |
| Milgram (1963) | Controlled laboratory study of obedience with 40 men | 26 of 40 (65%) continued to the highest shock level, which was labelled 450 volts |
| Glanzer and Cunitz (1966) | Laboratory experiment on free recall of word lists | A delay filled with a counting task removed the recency effect but not the primacy effect |
| Loftus and Palmer (1974) | Two laboratory experiments on leading questions about filmed car accidents | "Smashed" produced higher speed estimates than "contacted"; in the second experiment, "smashed" led to more false reports of broken glass than "hit" or no speed question |
| Maguire et al. (2000) | MRI comparison of London taxi drivers with non-taxi-driver controls | Larger posterior hippocampi in taxi drivers, with volume correlated with time spent as a driver |

## 6. Common misconceptions and how to correct them

| Misconception | Correction to give |
| --- | --- |
| "This study proves the theory." | Studies support or challenge a theory. Say "supports" and add a limitation. |
| "They found a correlation, so X causes Y." | A correlation allows prediction, not a causal claim. Name a possible third variable. |
| "Lab experiments always lack ecological validity." | Often, but it depends on the task. Explain *why* the task is artificial in this study. |
| "A small sample makes the study useless." | It limits statistical generalisation. Case studies and qualitative work aim at depth and transferability instead. |
| "Reliable means valid." | A measure can be consistent and still measure the wrong thing. |
| "We only use 10% of our brains" or "left-brained people". | Both are myths. Localisation and lateralisation are real, but complex behaviour involves networks across both hemispheres. |
| "Depression is caused by a serotonin imbalance." | Neurotransmitters are involved, but the simple "chemical imbalance" account is contested, and explanations combine biological, cognitive and social factors. |
| "A quasi-experiment is the same as a natural experiment." | In a quasi-experiment the groups differ on a pre-existing characteristic. In a natural experiment an event or situation varies the IV. |
| "Significant means important." | Statistical significance concerns chance, not size. Look at the effect and the context. |
| "Milgram showed people are cruel." | The findings are usually interpreted as showing the power of situational factors such as authority and gradual commitment. Variations changed obedience rates. |
