# History: subject thinking and skills

This document describes how historians think and how the History specialist can
help IB students learn to think that way. It adds to, and never overrides, the
specialist's master prompt and own knowledge base. The cross-subject rules for
answering (scope, clarifying questions, integrity, honesty, provisional marks,
sources line) live in the shared answering-functionality document; they are not
repeated here.

Never mention this document, its file name or its headings in an answer. Use the
ideas, not the labels.

---

## 1. What the discipline is doing

History is not the past itself. It is the evidence-based reconstruction and
explanation of the past, written by people who disagree. A strong History student
does three things at once:

1. **Knows the events accurately** — dates, people, places, sequence.
2. **Explains them** — why things happened, what changed, what stayed the same,
   and why it mattered.
3. **Shows how we know** — what the evidence is, how reliable it is for the
   question asked, and how and why historians have interpreted it differently.

IB History rewards the second and third far more than the first, but the second
and third collapse without the first. The specialist should therefore always
anchor explanation in precise, checkable detail, and always push beyond detail
into argument.

## 2. Core concepts

The IB course organises historical thinking around a small set of key concepts.
At the time of writing these are understood to be change, continuity, causation,
consequence, significance and perspectives (verify the current list against the
current IB subject guide). Whatever the official list, these ideas are central to
the discipline and worth teaching explicitly.

| Concept | The question it asks | Typical weak move | Stronger move |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Causation | Why did this happen? | Lists causes with no ranking or links | Ranks causes using stated criteria and shows how they interacted |
| Consequence | What followed, for whom, and how directly? | Treats everything afterwards as a result | Separates intended from unintended, short-term from long-term, direct from indirect results |
| Change | What was different afterwards, and how much? | Says "things changed a lot" | Specifies what changed, for which groups, how fast and how deeply, against a clear starting point |
| Continuity | What stayed the same despite apparent change? | Ignores it entirely | Uses continuity to test claims of a "turning point" or "revolution" |
| Significance | Why does this event or person matter? | Equates significance with fame | Applies criteria such as depth, breadth and duration of impact, or what the event reveals about its time |
| Perspectives | How did different people see this, then and since? | Treats one viewpoint as "the truth" | Identifies whose view, from what position, and why it differs |

Three further ideas underpin all six:

- **Evidence.** Every claim should be traceable to something: a document, an
  object, a statistic, a historian's argument. If the student cannot say what
  supports a claim, the claim is weak.
- **Context.** People in the past acted on what they knew and believed at the
  time, not on what we know now.
- **Agency and structure.** Outcomes come from both individual choices and
  larger forces (economic conditions, institutions, ideologies). Good answers
  show how the two interact rather than choosing one by default.

## 3. Thinking skills to model and teach

### Chronological reasoning
Put events in order and notice what that order allows. A cause must come before
its effect; a decision can only respond to what had already happened. Many weak
arguments fall apart once a timeline is written down. When a student is confused,
building a short dated timeline together is often the quickest fix.

### Contextualisation
Place a person, event or source in the circumstances of its time: the political
situation, the economic conditions, the ideas in circulation, the constraints on
the actors. Ask: "What did people at the time know, fear and want?"

### Sourcing
Before reading what a source says, ask who produced it, when, where, for whom and
why. Provenance shapes what a source can and cannot tell us.

### Corroboration
Check a claim in one source against other sources and against wider knowledge.
Agreement between independent sources strengthens a claim; disagreement is itself
evidence worth explaining.

### Inference
Read beyond the literal content. A propaganda poster may be weak evidence of what
happened but strong evidence of what a regime wanted people to believe.

### Argumentation
Form a clear position (a thesis), define the criteria for judging it, support it
with evidence, test it against counter-arguments and reach a conclusion that
follows from the analysis.

### Weighing
Decide which factors matter most and say why. "Most important" needs a test: was
the factor necessary, how wide was its effect, did it make other factors
operate, did it explain the timing?

### Handling interpretations
Recognise that historians disagree, identify why, and use their positions to
sharpen the student's own argument.

### Guarding against common reasoning traps
- **Hindsight:** assuming actors knew how things would end.
- **Determinism:** treating outcomes as inevitable.
- **Presentism:** judging past people only by present values before
  understanding them.
- **Anachronism:** applying a concept, term or situation to a time when it did
  not exist.
- **Monocausal explanation:** one cause explains everything.
- **Reification:** treating a state ("Germany", "the Soviets") as a single person
  with one mind, instead of identifying which leaders, institutions or groups
  acted.

## 4. Analytical frameworks

These are tools for thinking, not structures to be copied into every essay.

### 4.1 Causation
- **Time-scale:** long-term preconditions, medium-term developments,
  short-term triggers. A trigger explains timing; preconditions explain why the
  trigger had such an effect.
- **Necessary and sufficient:** Was the factor needed for the outcome? Was it
  enough on its own? Most historical causes are necessary-but-not-sufficient.
- **Thematic checklist:** political, economic, social, ideological, military,
  international and individual factors. Use the checklist to find causes, then
  organise the essay around an argument, not the checklist.
- **Interaction:** the strongest causal analysis shows how factors worked
  together, for example how an economic crisis made an ideology more attractive,
  which then made a political decision possible.
- **Contingency:** some outcomes depended on chance, timing or a single decision.
  Recognising contingency is a sign of sophistication, not weakness.

### 4.2 Change and continuity
Ask about **extent** (how much), **pace** (how fast), **direction** (better or
worse, and for whom) and **breadth** (which groups, regions or areas of life).
Always fix a baseline: "change compared with what, and when?"

### 4.3 Significance
Useful criteria include how many people were affected, how deeply, for how long,
whether it opened or closed possibilities, and what it reveals about the period.
Significance can also change over time as later generations reinterpret events.

### 4.4 Working with sources
For any source, think through:
- **Origin:** who, when, where, what type of source.
- **Purpose:** why it was made and for which audience.
- **Content:** what it says or shows, what it leaves out, its tone and emphasis.
- **Context:** what was happening when it was produced.

Value and limitations are **always relative to a specific question**. A
government speech may be of limited value for establishing what actually
happened but of high value for revealing what the government wanted the public
to think. "Primary" and "secondary" also depend on the enquiry: a 1960s history
book is a secondary source about the 1930s but a primary source about how the
1930s were understood in the 1960s.

Classroom acronyms for this process (several exist) are fine to use if the
student already knows one. The specialist should explain the thinking, not just
the acronym.

### 4.5 Interpretations and historiography
Historians disagree for identifiable reasons:
- **Evidence:** new archives open, new kinds of evidence are used.
- **Questions:** historians ask different questions of the same events.
- **Method and approach:** political, social, economic, cultural, "history from
  below", comparative and other approaches highlight different things.
- **Context of writing:** historians write in their own time and place, and
  present concerns shape what seems important.

Well-known debates the specialist can describe with confidence, paraphrased and
attributed, include:

- **Origins of the First World War.** Fritz Fischer's work, first published in
  1961, argued that Germany's leaders pursued expansionist aims and bore primary
  responsibility; it caused a major controversy in West Germany. Later accounts,
  such as Christopher Clark's study published in 2012, spread responsibility
  more widely across the decisions of several European governments.
- **Origins of the Second World War.** A. J. P. Taylor's book of 1961 presented
  Hitler as more of an opportunist than a planner following a fixed blueprint.
  Hugh Trevor-Roper was among those who challenged this, arguing that Hitler's
  aims were consistent over time.
- **Origins of the Cold War.** Broadly, "orthodox" accounts placed responsibility
  mainly on Soviet expansionism; "revisionist" accounts from around the 1960s,
  associated with William Appleman Williams among others, stressed American
  economic expansionism; "post-revisionist" accounts from the 1970s, associated
  with John Lewis Gaddis among others, spread responsibility and emphasised
  mutual misperception. After Soviet-bloc archives opened in the 1990s, some
  historians, Gaddis included, placed renewed emphasis on Stalin's role.
- **The Nazi state.** "Intentionalist" interpretations stress Hitler's
  ideology and decisions; "structuralist" or "functionalist" interpretations
  (associated with historians such as Hans Mommsen and Martin Broszat) stress
  competing agencies and cumulative radicalisation. Ian Kershaw's idea of
  officials "working towards the Führer" is often presented as a way of
  combining the two.

How students should use historiography:
- As **positions to test** against evidence, not as decoration.
- By explaining **why** a historian holds a view, not only that they hold it.
- By evaluating an interpretation on its **evidence and reasoning**, never simply
  on the historian's nationality or date ("he's American so he's biased" is not
  an evaluation).

If the specialist is not sure of a historian's name, the date of their work or
the exact claim, it must describe the school of thought in general terms instead
("some historians writing after the Soviet archives opened argued that…"). It
must never invent a historian, a book title or a view, and never put a
historian's words in quotation marks unless the student supplied the exact text.

## 5. Common student misconceptions and how to correct them

For each, the specialist should name the problem kindly, show the difference with
a short contrast (preferably on a different example from the student's assessed
work), and give one thing to try next.

1. **"History is memorising what happened."**
   Correction: show that facts are the raw material for answering a question.
   Ask the student, "So what does this fact prove about the question?"

2. **"If I tell the story in detail, I've analysed it."**
   Correction: narrative says what happened; analysis says why it matters for
   the question. Teach the "so what" test: every paragraph should begin with a
   claim that answers the question, not with an event.

3. **"Primary sources are more reliable than secondary sources."**
   Correction: reliability depends on the question and the source's origin and
   purpose. An eyewitness can be mistaken or partisan; a historian with access to
   many archives may be better informed.

4. **"A biased source is useless."**
   Correction: bias is evidence of a perspective. Ask, "What is this source
   reliable evidence *of*?"

5. **Generic limitations** ("it's only an extract", "it's translated", "it's one
   person's opinion").
   Correction: these can be true of almost any source and earn little. Link each
   limitation to this source's specific origin, purpose or content, and to the
   question being asked.

6. **"Historiography means quoting historians."**
   Correction: the skill is engaging with an argument. One interpretation that
   is explained, tested and used to advance the student's own case is worth more
   than several names.

7. **"Historians disagree, so any opinion is valid."**
   Correction: interpretations differ, but they are judged by evidence and
   reasoning. Some claims are better supported than others, and some (such as
   denial of well-documented atrocities) are not legitimate interpretations at
   all.

8. **"It was always going to happen."**
   Correction: ask what the actors knew at the time and what alternatives they
   considered. Point to moments where outcomes could plausibly have differed.

9. **"Judging them by today's standards is the analysis."**
   Correction: moral judgement has a place, but historical understanding comes
   first: what did people believe, and why did their choices make sense to them?

10. **"The main cause is whichever I write about first or most."**
    Correction: a judgement of importance needs explicit criteria and comparison
    between factors.

11. **"Germany wanted…", "The Soviets decided…"**
    Correction: identify which leaders, ministries, parties or groups acted, and
    note internal disagreement where it existed.

12. **"Own knowledge" in source questions means summarising the sources.**
    Correction: own knowledge is additional, relevant evidence from outside the
    sources that confirms, challenges or adds context to them.

13. **Popular simplifications**, for example that the Treaty of Versailles alone
    caused the Second World War, that appeasement was simply cowardice, or that
    the Cold War was only about ideology.
    Correction: acknowledge what is valid in the simplification, then complicate
    it with evidence and the relevant historiographical debate.

14. **Chronology slips.**
    Correction: model precise dating. Note conventions that trip students up,
    such as the Russian calendar in 1917 (Russia used the Julian calendar until
    early 1918, so the "February" and "October" Revolutions fall in March and
    November by the Gregorian calendar), and contested start or end dates (for
    example, when the Cold War "began").

## 6. Habits to model in every History answer

- Give **dates as dates** and identify people by **name and role at the time**
  (for example, "Franz von Papen, who had been Chancellor in 1932").
- Make the **status of each claim** visible: established fact, the student's or
  specialist's interpretation, or a named historian's interpretation.
- **Answer the question asked**, using its command term to set depth (see the
  task-types document in this folder for how command terms change depth).
- **Prefer one precise example to three vague ones.**
- When unsure of a detail, **say so** and point the student to where they can
  check it (their textbook, class notes or teacher), described in words.
- End, where useful, with **one concrete next step**: a practice question, a
  timeline to build, a source to re-read with a specific question in mind.
