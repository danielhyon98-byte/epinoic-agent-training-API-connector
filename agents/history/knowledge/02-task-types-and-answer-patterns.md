# History: task types and answer patterns

This document describes the kinds of History tasks IB students bring, how the
specialist should approach each, and how to give provisional, criterion-based
feedback. It is generic on purpose. Paper structures, question formats, mark
totals and assessment criteria change between editions of the IB subject guide,
so anything course-specific below is marked for verification. Where the
specialist's own knowledge base describes the current course, follow it.

Integrity rules, provisional-mark wording and the sources line are set out in the
shared answering-functionality document and in this folder's answering document;
this file applies them to History tasks.

---

## 1. How command terms set depth

The command term tells the student, and the specialist, what kind of thinking is
wanted. The same topic can need a two-line answer or a fully argued essay
depending on the verb. The descriptions below are written in plain words for
this supplement; they are not the IB's official definitions.

### Recall and description
| Command term | What it asks for, in plain words | A strong response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| State | A short, specific answer: a name, date, figure or fact | Is precise and stops there |
| Identify | Pick out the relevant item or items | Names them clearly, with minimal elaboration |
| Outline | Sketch the main points briefly | Covers the key points in a logical order without development |
| Describe | Say what something was like or what happened, with detail | Gives accurate, relevant detail; reasons are not required |

### Explanation and analysis
| Command term | What it asks for, in plain words | A strong response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explain | Show why or how something happened | Links causes to effects with clear reasoning and specific evidence |
| Analyse | Separate something into its parts and show how they work and which matter most | Breaks the issue into components and shows relationships between them |
| Examine | Probe what lies beneath a claim and how its parts connect | Questions the premise, looks at different dimensions, reaches a view |
| Compare / contrast / compare and contrast | Show where two or more things are alike, differ, or both | Works point by point, keeping both items in view in each point, rather than describing one and then the other |

### Judgement
| Command term | What it asks for, in plain words | A strong response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Evaluate | Judge how far something holds up, setting what supports it against what undermines it | Weighs both sides and reaches an explicit, justified verdict |
| Discuss | Explore several sides or factors fairly, then commit to a reasoned conclusion | Balanced coverage plus a clear final position |
| To what extent | Measure how far a claim is true | Gives a degree ("largely", "only partly", "hardly at all"), argued with evidence and limits |
| Justify | Back a position with reasons and evidence | Gives a clear claim and the grounds for it |

### One topic, three depths
- *"Outline the military terms of the Treaty of Versailles."* A short list:
  army limited to 100,000 men, no conscription, the Rhineland demilitarised,
  heavy restrictions on the navy and a ban on an air force.
- *"Explain why many Germans resented the Treaty of Versailles."* Reasons with
  mechanism: the sense of a dictated peace, the war-guilt clause, territorial
  losses, reparations, and why each touched German politics in the early 1920s.
- *"To what extent was the Treaty of Versailles responsible for the outbreak of
  war in 1939?"* An argued judgement that weighs the treaty against later factors
  such as the Depression, Hitler's aims and the international response, and
  states how far the claim holds.

If the student gives a question without its command term, or paraphrases it,
the specialist should ask for the exact wording when the depth genuinely matters
(see the answering document in this folder).

## 2. Task types and how to approach them

### 2.1 Short factual and definition questions
*Examples: "When was the Enabling Act passed?" "What was the Truman Doctrine?"*

- Answer first, precisely: date, name, place.
- Add one or two sentences of context so the fact is usable.
- If the term is contested or has several meanings, say so briefly.
- If the student seems to be revising, offer a quick follow-up question to test
  recall.

### 2.2 Explanation questions
*Examples: "Why did the Weimar Republic struggle in 1923?" "How did Stalin
consolidate power after Lenin's death?"*

- Lead with a one-sentence answer that names the main reasons.
- Develop each reason: what happened, the evidence, and how it produced the
  outcome.
- Show links between reasons where they exist.
- Separate established fact from interpretation, and attribute interpretations.
- Avoid drifting into an essay with a full counter-argument unless asked; an
  explanation question needs reasons, not necessarily a verdict.

### 2.3 Source-based tasks
In the current course these sit mainly in a source-based paper on prescribed
subjects (verify against the current IB subject guide). Students also meet them
in class tests and in the internal assessment. Common question shapes are:

**Comprehension or message.** Questions such as "What can we learn from Source A
about…?" or "What point is the cartoonist making?"
- Work only from the source the student has supplied. If the source is not in
  the conversation, ask for it; do not guess its content.
- For text: identify the main points relevant to the question, in the student's
  own words, with brief reference to the text.
- For visual sources: the specialist cannot see images in the text-only pilot.
  Ask the student to describe exactly what is shown (figures, symbols, labels,
  caption, publication and date), then help them read the message.

**Value and limitations.** Questions that ask how useful a source is to a
historian investigating a stated topic, judged by who made it, why, and what it
contains.
- Keep the stated enquiry in view throughout; value is always "value *for* this
  question".
- Use specific details of origin (who, when, what type), purpose (why, for
  whom) and content (what it says, omits, emphasises, its tone).
- Avoid generic points that apply to any source.
- Balance value and limitations; neither should be a token sentence.

**Comparison.** Questions that ask where two sources agree and disagree about
an issue.
- Organise point by point, not source by source.
- Find genuine, specific similarities and differences tied to the question.
- Refer to the content of both sources in each point.

**Synthesis with own knowledge.** Questions that ask the student to reach a
judgement on a claim, drawing on a set of sources together with what they know
from their studies.
- This is a short argued essay. Give a clear judgement.
- Use the sources as evidence (referred to by their labels) and add relevant own
  knowledge that the sources do not contain.
- Integrate sources into the argument rather than summarising each in turn.

### 2.4 Essays
In the current course, essays appear on world history topics and, at HL, on a
regional option; some essay questions ask for examples from more than one region
(verify against the current IB subject guide). Students also write essays for
class assessment.

**What strong History essays contain**
- **A clear thesis** in the introduction that directly answers the question,
  including the degree of agreement where the command term requires it.
- **Defined terms and scope**: what the key words mean, what period and which
  places are covered.
- **Analytical structure**: paragraphs organised around arguments or factors,
  not a chronological retelling. Chronological structure can work for change
  questions if each section makes a judgement.
- **Paragraph anatomy**: a claim that answers the question, specific evidence
  (dates, names, figures, events), analysis that explains how the evidence
  supports the claim, and a link back to the question.
- **Counter-argument**: the strongest opposing case, treated fairly, then
  answered or conceded in part.
- **Historiography where it helps**: an interpretation used to sharpen the
  argument, attributed accurately.
- **A conclusion** that follows from the analysis and does not introduce new
  material or change the thesis.

**How the specialist helps**
- For practice questions: discuss the question's demands, help the student build
  a thesis and plan, suggest evidence they could use, and model a paragraph on a
  *different* question if a model would help.
- For essays that will be graded (coursework or class assessments): plan,
  question and give feedback on the student's own writing; do not write the
  essay. If it is unclear whether an essay is graded, ask.
- For timed exam practice: help the student plan quickly (thesis, three or four
  argued sections, counter-argument, conclusion) and judge what to leave out.

### 2.5 The historical investigation (internal assessment)
The internal assessment in History is a historical investigation. In the current
course it is understood to involve evaluating sources, an investigation that
answers a research question, and a reflection on the methods and challenges
historians face, within a word limit (verify the current structure, word limit
and criteria against the current IB subject guide).

**What the specialist may do**
- Help the student choose and narrow a topic and sharpen their own research
  question (for example, by asking what period, place, actors and type of
  judgement they have in mind).
- Explain what makes a research question workable: focused, answerable with
  available evidence, phrased to invite a judgement.
- Explain how to evaluate sources and test the student's reasoning about sources
  *they* have chosen, by asking questions rather than supplying finished
  evaluations.
- Describe kinds of sources to look for in words (published document
  collections, newspaper archives, memoirs, official records, scholarly
  monographs) without inventing specific titles or giving web addresses.
- Give feedback on drafts the student has written, and provisional marks where
  appropriate.
- Prompt reflection with questions about the student's own research process.

**What the specialist must not do**
- Write any section, paragraph or source evaluation for submission.
- Rewrite the student's draft into polished, submittable prose.
- Invent sources, archives or historians for the student to cite.

**Common pitfalls to flag**
- A research question that is too broad for the word limit.
- An investigation that narrates rather than argues.
- Source evaluation not linked to the research question.
- A reflection that is generic rather than drawn from the student's own
  experience of the investigation.
- Topics too recent for the course rules (verify any time limit on recent
  events against the current IB subject guide).

### 2.6 The extended essay in History
Similar principles apply: the student owns the research question, the argument
and the writing. The specialist can help narrow scope, think about evidence,
plan structure and respond to drafts. Extended essay criteria differ from
History course criteria and have changed between editions (verify against the
current extended essay guide). If the student's extended essay is in a different
subject or is interdisciplinary, help only with the historical content and say
so.

### 2.7 Feedback on drafts
See section 4. Ask what the task is (the exact question and, if relevant, the
criteria) before giving a number. Feedback without a number is always available.

### 2.8 Revision and exam technique
- **Timelines and retrieval:** build a dated timeline with the student, then
  quiz them on it.
- **Practice questions:** write original practice questions in the style of the
  course, clearly labelled as practice and not as past-paper questions.
- **Factor grids:** help the student list factors for a causation question and
  rank them with criteria.
- **Historiography cards:** for each topic, a short note on one or two
  interpretations they can explain confidently.
- **Model on a different example:** if the student needs to see a technique,
  demonstrate it on a different question from the one they are practising.

## 3. What strong History answers have in common

Use this as a final check before sending any substantive answer.

- It answers the question asked, at the depth the command term requires.
- The main point comes first.
- Claims are supported by specific, accurate evidence: dates, names, places,
  numbers with their context.
- Fact, interpretation and historiography are distinguishable.
- Any historian named is real, correctly attributed and paraphrased, not quoted.
- Nothing is quoted unless the exact words were supplied by the student.
- Uncertainty is stated honestly.
- It is complete: no part of the request is silently dropped.
- It ends with a useful next step where that helps learning.

## 4. Giving provisional, criterion-based feedback

### 4.1 Before marking
1. **Identify the task:** the exact question or prompt, the component (for
   example an essay, a source question, the internal assessment) and, if it
   matters, SL or HL.
2. **Identify the criteria:** use the criteria the student or their teacher has
   supplied, or those in the specialist's own knowledge base. If neither is
   available, either ask which criteria apply or give qualitative feedback
   without a number. Never invent official-sounding criteria.
3. **Check scope:** a single paragraph cannot honestly receive a whole-essay
   mark. Say what part of the work the judgement covers.

### 4.2 Reading the work
Read in this order:
1. **Argument:** Is there a clear answer to the question? Does it hold together?
2. **Evidence:** Is it specific, accurate and relevant? Flag factual errors
   precisely and correct them.
3. **Analysis:** Does the student explain how evidence supports claims, or just
   place them side by side?
4. **Balance and interpretation:** Is there a counter-argument? Is
   historiography used, and used well?
5. **Structure and clarity:** Does each paragraph advance the argument?

### 4.3 Writing the feedback
A reliable shape:
- **What works** — one or two specific strengths, pointing to the student's own
  words.
- **What would move it up** — two or three improvements, ordered by impact, each
  with a concrete action ("add the date and the specific clause", "turn your
  first sentence into a claim that answers the question").
- **Provisional judgement** — only if the criteria and scope allow it. The word
  "provisional" must be in the same sentence as any mark, band or level, with a
  reminder that only the teacher and the IB award real marks.
- **Next step** — one task the student can do now.

Do not rewrite the student's work for them. Showing a technique on one sentence
from a different topic is fine; returning a polished version of their paragraph
is not.

### 4.4 An illustrative feedback rubric

The rubric below is **illustrative, not the official IB criteria**. Use it only
when no actual criteria are available and the student wants a structured,
qualitative picture. Do not convert it into a numerical IB mark.

| Dimension | Developing | Secure | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Focus and argument | Mostly narrative; question addressed indirectly | Clear thesis, mostly sustained | Clear, nuanced thesis sustained throughout, with a justified conclusion |
| Knowledge and accuracy | General or partly inaccurate | Accurate and relevant, some specific detail | Precise, well-chosen detail used as evidence |
| Analysis and evaluation | Assertions with little explanation | Explains links between evidence and claims | Weighs factors or sources and explains why some matter more |
| Perspectives and interpretations | Single viewpoint | Acknowledges other views | Uses interpretations critically to advance the argument |

### 4.5 Expressing a provisional mark
- Use the scale the student or the criteria supply. If the student says their
  teacher marks essays out of a given total, it is acceptable to use that scale
  while making clear the estimate is provisional.
- Put the label in the same sentence as the number, for example: "Provisional
  estimate: around 9 out of 15 if the whole essay were at this level; only your
  teacher and the IB award real marks."
- Prefer a range over false precision when judging part of a piece.
- Never inflate a mark to encourage, and never present a mark as what the IB
  "would" award.

### 4.6 Things to avoid
- Generic praise ("good job", "nice analysis") without pointing to the text.
- Marking against criteria for the wrong component.
- Feedback so long the student cannot act on it. Prioritise.
- Silently correcting facts. Name the error and give the correct fact so the
  student learns it.
