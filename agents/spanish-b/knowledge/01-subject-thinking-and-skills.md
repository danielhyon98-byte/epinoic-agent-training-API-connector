# Language B: Spanish: subject thinking and skills

This is background for the tutor, not text to show students. It sits under your
master prompt and your own knowledge base. General answering rules shared by every
specialist are in the shared answering document; this file covers the subject.

## 1. What a language-acquisition course is

Spanish B is a **language-acquisition** course. Students who already have some
Spanish develop the ability to understand and communicate in it, accurately and
appropriately, for real purposes and audiences, and build understanding of the
Spanish-speaking world. It is not a literature course in Spanish (that is a
Language A course), and it is not a beginners' course (that is ab initio).

At HL, students meet more complex texts and also study literary works in Spanish
(verify the number of works and how they are assessed against the current IB subject
guide). The course is organised around broad themes, currently along the lines of
identity, experiences, human ingenuity, social organisation and sharing the planet
(verify the current theme names against the current IB subject guide). Themes are
contexts for using language, not content to memorise.

**The subject is the course, not the language of the message.** A student may write
to you in English or in Spanish. "How do I use the subjunctive?" in English is a
Spanish B request; "¿Me explicas la fotosíntesis?" in Spanish is a Biology request.

## 2. Three kinds of skill

The course is often described in terms of three kinds of communication (verify the
framing against the current IB subject guide):

- **Receptive: reading and listening.** Understanding gist and detail, inferring
  attitude and purpose, recognising how a text is organised, and matching ideas
  that are paraphrased rather than repeated word for word.
- **Productive: writing and speaking.** Producing a text for a stated audience and
  purpose, in the right text type and register, with ideas developed and organised.
- **Interactive: conversation.** Responding to questions, sustaining an exchange,
  asking for clarification, defending an opinion, and recovering when a word is
  missing ("¿Cómo se dice…?", "Lo que quiero decir es que…").

A good tutor practises all three. In a text-only conversation, listening practice is
limited to strategies and to transcripts the student pastes; you cannot play or hear
audio.

## 3. Text types and register

Every productive task has four elements: **message, audience, purpose, text type**.
Register follows from them.

Text types are often grouped as personal (diary, personal blog, informal email or
letter), professional (formal letter or email, report, proposal) and mass media
(article, review, opinion column, interview, speech, leaflet, news report). Verify
the grouping and the list against the current IB subject guide.

### Register in Spanish

| Feature | Informal | Formal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Address | tú / vosotros (Spain); ustedes for plural in Latin America | usted / ustedes |
| Greeting | "¡Hola, Lucía!" / "Querida Lucía:" | "Estimada señora directora:" / "Estimado señor:" |
| Closing | "Un abrazo," / "Besos," / "Hasta pronto," | "Atentamente," / "Le saluda atentamente," |
| Typical phrasing | "Te escribo para contarte…" | "Me dirijo a usted para…" |

Spanish letter greetings are usually followed by a colon, not a comma.

**Formal opening (example):**
> Estimada señora directora:
> Me dirijo a usted para expresarle mi preocupación por la falta de zonas verdes en
> nuestro barrio.

**Informal opening (example):**
> ¡Hola, Lucía!
> ¿Qué tal todo? Te escribo para contarte cómo me fue en el voluntariado este verano.

Mixing registers ("Estimado señor: ¿Qué tal?") is one of the commonest problems in
student writing. So is a text type reduced to a heading: a blog post needs a voice
and reader engagement, an article needs a headline and a clear angle, a speech needs
direct address to listeners.

## 4. Grammar areas students commonly struggle with

### Ser and estar
- *Ser* for identity, characteristics, origin, time and where an event takes place:
  "Mi hermano es aburrido" (he is a boring person). "La fiesta es en mi casa."
- *Estar* for states, conditions and location of people and things: "Mi hermano está
  aburrido" (he is bored right now). "Estoy listo" (I'm ready), but "Soy listo" (I'm
  clever).

### Por and para
- *Para*: purpose, destination, deadline, recipient. "Estudio para ser médica." "El
  regalo es para ti." "Necesito el informe para el lunes."
- *Por*: cause, exchange, means, movement through, duration. "Gracias por tu ayuda."
  "Lo compré por diez euros." "Paseamos por el parque." "Hablamos por teléfono."

### Preterite and imperfect
- Imperfect for background, description and habits: "Cuando era pequeña, vivía en un
  pueblo y todos los veranos íbamos a la playa."
- Preterite for completed events: "Un día, mi abuelo me regaló una bicicleta."
- Together: "Leía cuando sonó el teléfono." (an ongoing action interrupted)

### The subjunctive
Students often think of it as a tense. It is a mood, triggered by meaning.
- Wishes and influence: "Quiero que vengas." "Te recomiendo que leas el artículo."
- Emotion: "Me alegra que estés aquí."
- Doubt and negated opinion: "No creo que sea justo", but "Creo que es justo."
- Value judgements: "Es importante que los jóvenes voten."
- Future time after *cuando*: "Cuando termine el instituto, viajaré por Sudamérica."
  Compare the habitual "Cuando termino las clases, voy al gimnasio."
- Unknown or non-existent antecedent: "Busco un piso que tenga balcón."
- Hypotheses: "Si tuviera más tiempo, aprendería a tocar la guitarra."
- *Ojalá*: "Ojalá haga sol mañana."

### Verbs like gustar
The thing liked is the subject. "Me gusta la música." "Me gustan los idiomas." "A mis
padres les encanta viajar." Common errors: "Yo gusto el fútbol" (should be "Me gusta
el fútbol"); "Me gusta los deportes" (should be "Me gustan los deportes").

### Agreement
- Some nouns ending in *-ma* are masculine: el problema, el tema, el sistema, el
  clima, el idioma. And *la mano* is feminine: "las manos sucias".
- *La gente* is grammatically singular: "La gente es amable", not "La gente son
  amables".

### Written accents
Accents change meaning: tú (you) / tu (your), él (he) / el (the), sí (yes) / si (if),
sé (I know) / se, más (more) / mas (but, rare), té (tea) / te. Question words carry
accents: qué, cómo, dónde, cuándo, por qué (but the answer is *porque*). In verbs,
"hablo" (I speak) and "habló" (he or she spoke) are different words.

### Object pronouns
*Le* and *les* become *se* before *lo, la, los, las*: "Se lo di" (I gave it to him,
her or them).
Pronouns go before a conjugated verb or attach to an infinitive: "Lo quiero comprar"
or "Quiero comprarlo".

### False friends and calques
| Student writes | Problem | Natural Spanish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Estoy embarazada" (meaning embarrassed) | *Embarazada* means pregnant | "Me da vergüenza" / "Estoy avergonzado/a" |
| "Realicé que…" | *Realizar* means to carry out | "Me di cuenta de que…" |
| "Aplicar para un trabajo" | Calque from English | "Solicitar un puesto de trabajo" |
| "Hace sentido" | Calque | "Tiene sentido" |
| "Soy diecisiete años" | Age uses *tener* | "Tengo diecisiete años" |
| "En el otro lado" (on the other hand) | Wrong fixed phrase | "Por otro lado" |
| "La librería" (meaning library) | *Librería* is a bookshop | "La biblioteca" |

### Prepositions and the personal *a*
Verbs with fixed prepositions: pensar en, soñar con, depender de, enamorarse de,
casarse con, consistir en. The personal *a* before people: "Visité a mi abuela", but
"Visité el museo".

### Punctuation and capitals
Opening ¿ and ¡. Lower case for days, months, nationalities and languages: "el
lunes", "en enero", "los mexicanos", "hablo inglés".

### Connectors
además, sin embargo, no obstante, por lo tanto, por eso, en cambio, es decir, por un
lado… por otro (lado)…, a pesar de, aunque, en primer lugar, para concluir. Range and
accuracy of connectors are among the quickest gains in student writing.

## 5. Cultural and thematic discussion

- **The Spanish-speaking world is plural.** Spanish is an official language in around
  twenty countries and is widely spoken elsewhere, including the United States.
  Avoid treating "Latin America" or "Spain" as single cultures; be specific about
  place, and say "in some regions" when practices vary.
- **Varieties are equally valid.** Accept *vosotros* and *ustedes*, *voseo*,
  *ordenador* and *computadora*, *coche*, *carro* and *auto*, *móvil* and *celular*.
  Ask students to be consistent within a text, and to follow their teacher's
  preference where one is stated.
- **Facts follow the usual honesty rules.** Dates, figures and names about the
  Spanish-speaking world must be ones you are confident of. Say when you are not
  sure, and never invent statistics. Current figures (populations, numbers of
  speakers, office-holders, recent events) may be out of date: say they are
  approximate and as of your training.
- **Compare, do not rank.** Intercultural understanding means explaining practices
  and perspectives, comparing them with the student's own, and avoiding stereotypes.
- **Themes need language.** Help students build vocabulary for each theme, practise
  giving and justifying opinions ("Desde mi punto de vista…", "Estoy de acuerdo en
  parte, pero…"), and prepare concrete examples.

## 6. Common misconceptions and how to correct them

| Misconception | How to correct it |
| --- | --- |
| "I can translate word for word from English." | Show a calque next to the natural phrase, and teach set phrases as whole units. |
| "Harder vocabulary is always better." | Register and audience come first. An informal blog full of formal phrases reads as wrong. |
| "The subjunctive is a tense, or just for formal writing." | It is a mood triggered by meaning, used constantly in everyday speech. Teach the triggers. |
| "Accents are optional." | Show a pair where the accent changes the meaning: "hablo" and "habló". |
| "Spanish punctuation is the same as English." | Opening ¿ and ¡; colon after letter greetings; lower-case days and nationalities. |
| "Only Spain's Spanish is correct." | All standard varieties are correct. Consistency within a text is what matters. |
| "A text type is just a heading." | Each text type has a voice, a structure and conventions. A heading alone does not make an article. |
| "Culture means a list of facts about festivals." | Explore practices and perspectives, compare with the student's own culture, and avoid stereotypes. |
