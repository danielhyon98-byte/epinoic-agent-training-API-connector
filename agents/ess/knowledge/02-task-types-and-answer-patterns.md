# Environmental Systems and Societies: task types and answer patterns

This document covers the kinds of request an ESS student brings and the answer
pattern that works for each. It does not describe the official paper structure,
timings, mark totals or the levels at which the course is offered. If those matter,
tell the student to check the current subject guide or their teacher.

## 1. Multiple-choice reasoning

Whether ESS external assessment uses multiple-choice items is a course-structure fact
not stated here (verify against the current IB subject guide). Students still meet
multiple-choice questions in class tests and revision materials.

**Approach**

1. Identify the concept being tested (a systems term, a feedback type, a
   calculation).
2. Watch for options that confuse paired terms: transfer and transformation,
   positive and negative feedback, mitigation and adaptation, richness and evenness.
3. Explain why the correct option is right and why each distractor fails.

**Example.** "Planting trees to absorb carbon dioxide" is mitigation, because it
addresses the cause by reducing greenhouse gas concentrations. "Building flood
defences" is adaptation, because it reduces harm from the effects.

## 2. Short and structured answers

- Read the command term and mark allocation together. Marks suggest how many
  distinct points are expected.
- Use ESS vocabulary precisely and apply it to the context given.
- For "outline" or "explain" questions about systems, name the storage, flow or
  process involved.

## 3. Data-based and resource-based questions

ESS questions often supply unfamiliar resources: maps, graphs, tables, photographs
or short texts about a place or issue.

**Approach**

1. Read the resource's title, source description, units, scale and date range.
2. Describe patterns with figures from the resource, including exceptions.
3. Explain using systems ideas and environmental science.
4. Where asked to evaluate, bring in perspectives and EVS, and consider reliability
   and bias in the resource.
5. Link back to sustainability or management when the question invites it.

Never invent data or describe a resource the student has not supplied. If they
mention a figure or table you cannot see, explain the method and ask for the values.

## 4. Calculations

**Pattern (use every time)**

1. State the formula, including which version your course uses where versions
   differ.
2. Define each symbol.
3. Givens with units.
4. Explicit substitution, one step per line.
5. Result with unit (or "no unit" for indices) and sensible rounding.
6. Interpret the number in words.

**Lincoln index (invented data).** In the first sample, 40 animals are caught and
marked (M = 40). In the second sample, 50 are caught (C = 50), of which 8 are marked
(R = 8).
N = (M × C) / R = (40 × 50) / 8 = 2000 / 8 = 250 individuals
Assumptions to state: marks are not lost and do not affect survival; marked animals
mix fully into the population; the population is closed (no births, deaths or
migration) between samples.

**Other common calculations:** Simpson's diversity index, energy transfer
efficiency, gross and net productivity, percentage change, population growth rate
and doubling time, and ecological footprint compared with biocapacity.

## 5. Extended responses ("discuss", "evaluate", "to what extent")

**Approach**

1. Define key terms in the question briefly.
2. Use the systems approach to frame the issue.
3. Develop several points, each supported by an example or evidence, and say where
   the evidence is from (the student's case study, a resource provided, or general
   understanding).
4. Bring in at least two EVS perspectives and evaluate them.
5. Reach a clear, justified conclusion that answers "to what extent" with a degree,
   not just "yes" or "no".

**What strong answers contain:** clear structure, accurate terminology, specific and
accurate examples, a diagram described where helpful, and a balanced judgement.

## 6. Practical work, fieldwork and internal assessment guidance

Help the student plan, check and improve their own investigation; never write text
for submission (see this specialist's answering-functionality document).

- A focused research question links a measurable variable to an environmental issue
  in a defined place or system.
- Choose a sampling strategy and justify it (random, systematic, stratified).
- Plan enough samples to be representative; record abiotic and biotic data with
  units.
- Consider ethics: minimise disturbance to organisms and habitats, obtain consent
  for surveys of people, and protect respondents' anonymity.
- In evaluation, distinguish limitations of method from limitations of scope, and
  connect findings to the wider issue and possible management responses.

Official IA criterion names, weightings and length limits are not stated here.
Verify them against the current IB subject guide.

## 7. Feedback on drafts and answers

- Point to the student's words or working.
- Fix errors of science or terminology first (for example, feedback direction,
  albedo, mitigation versus adaptation), then argument and balance, then style.
- Give two or three priorities with the specific change for each.
- Any mark is labelled provisional in the same sentence.

## 8. Command-term depth

Paraphrases for tutoring; the official definitions are in the current subject guide.

| Command term | What the answer must do |
| --- | --- |
| State, identify, list | Give specific facts or names without explanation. |
| Define | Give the precise meaning of a term. |
| Outline | Give the main points briefly. |
| Describe | Give a detailed account of a pattern, process or feature. |
| Explain | Give reasons or mechanisms. |
| Calculate | Reach a numerical answer with working. |
| Distinguish, compare and contrast | Give differences (and similarities where asked), referring to both throughout. |
| Suggest | Propose a plausible answer where more than one may be valid. |
| Justify | Support a choice or conclusion with evidence or reasoning. |
| Discuss, evaluate | Weigh strengths, limitations and perspectives, and reach a judgement. |
| To what extent | Weigh the case for and against and conclude how far a claim holds. |

## 9. Provisional criterion-based feedback

**Exam-style questions with a stated mark allocation:** build illustrative mark
points (labelled "not the official markscheme"), say which the student earned and
what is missing, and label the number provisional in the same sentence, for example:
"Provisional mark: 3 out of 4 — only your teacher and the IB award real marks." Every
sentence that awards a mark or sub-mark carries its own "provisional" label. With no
allocation, give qualitative feedback or ask for it.

**Extended responses and investigation drafts:** when the student has not supplied
the official criteria, use this rubric. It is illustrative, not the official IB
criteria.

| Aspect (illustrative) | Developing | Secure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Knowledge and terminology | Some terms misused or undefined. | Accurate terminology applied to the context. |
| Systems thinking | Issue described in isolation. | Storages, flows and feedback identified and linked across scales. |
| Evidence and examples | General or unsupported claims. | Specific, accurate examples or data supporting each point. |
| Perspectives and values | One viewpoint only. | Two or more EVS perspectives evaluated fairly. |
| Judgement | Missing or unsupported. | Clear, justified conclusion that answers the question as asked. |

Give band-style comments, each labelled provisional in the same sentence
("Provisionally, this is closer to Developing for method because …"), rather than a
number unless the student supplies the official criteria; any number is then
provisional, labelled in the same sentence too.
