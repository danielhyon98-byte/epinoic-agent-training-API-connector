# Geography: subject thinking and skills

This document describes how geographers think and how the Geography specialist
can help IB students learn to think that way. It adds to, and never overrides,
the specialist's master prompt and own knowledge base. The cross-subject
answering rules are in the shared answering-functionality document and are not
repeated here. Never mention this document, its file name or its headings in an
answer.

---

## 1. What the discipline is doing

Geography studies **places**, the **processes** that shape them, and the
**interactions between people and environments**, at every scale from a street
to the whole planet. IB Geography combines physical and human geography and
focuses on contemporary issues. Strong students:

1. **Describe patterns accurately**, with data, units and named places.
2. **Explain them** through physical and human processes, and the links between
   the two.
3. **Evaluate** issues and strategies from different perspectives and at
   different scales, reaching a supported judgement.

---

## 2. Core concepts

The IB course frames geographic thinking around a small set of key concepts. At
the time of writing these are understood to include place, process, power and
possibility, with scale and spatial interaction also central (verify the current
list against the current IB subject guide). The ideas below are worth teaching
explicitly whatever the official list.

| Concept | The question it asks |
| --- | --- |
| Place | What makes this location distinctive, and how do people experience it differently? |
| Space and spatial interaction | Where are things, why there, and how do flows of people, goods, money and ideas connect places? |
| Scale | Does the pattern or explanation change from local to national to global, or from days to decades? |
| Process | What physical or human mechanisms produce this pattern? |
| Power | Who makes decisions, who benefits, and who bears the costs? |
| Possibility | What futures are plausible, and what would change the outcome? |
| Systems | What are the inputs, stores, flows, outputs and feedback loops? |
| Sustainability | Can this continue without undermining environmental, social or economic well-being? |
| Inequality and diversity | How do outcomes differ between and within places and groups? |

## 3. Thinking skills

- **Spatial thinking.** Look for distributions, clusters, gradients, corridors
  and outliers, and ask why the pattern takes that shape. Distance, accessibility
  and connectivity often explain more than students expect.
- **Scale-shifting.** Test any explanation at more than one spatial and temporal
  scale. A city can be growing while its inner districts lose population.
- **Systems thinking.** Model a drainage basin, a coastline or a city as a system
  with inputs, stores, transfers and outputs. Look for positive feedback (which
  amplifies change) and negative feedback (which dampens it), and for thresholds.
- **Synthesis of human and physical.** Many of the best answers show how physical
  processes and human decisions interact: a flood is a rainfall event and a
  land-use and planning outcome.
- **Data literacy.** Read maps, graphs, tables and images precisely; quote values
  with units; identify trends, rates of change and anomalies; question how data
  was collected and what it leaves out.
- **Quantitative reasoning.** Percentages and percentage change, rates per 1,000,
  densities, means and medians, ranges and interquartile ranges, and simple
  statistical tests where appropriate. Always show working.
- **Perspectives and stakeholders.** Identify who is affected, how their
  interests differ and how much power each has.
- **Evaluating strategies.** Judge effectiveness by asking: effective for whom,
  at what scale, over what time period, at what cost, and with what side effects?
- **Fieldwork reasoning.** Turn a question into something measurable, choose a
  sampling strategy, collect reliable data, and recognise the limits of the
  method.

## 4. Analytical frameworks

Use these as tools, and always mention their limitations when evaluating.

- **Systems models.** The drainage basin (precipitation, interception,
  infiltration, throughflow, groundwater flow, surface runoff, channel flow),
  the sediment cell on coasts, and the carbon and water cycles.
- **Hazard and disaster risk.** Risk depends on the hazard, on exposure, on the
  vulnerability of people and places, and on their capacity to cope. Courses
  often summarise this as a simple equation. The key insight is that a disaster
  is a social outcome, not only a physical event.
- **Development and inequality measures.** Income per head, composite indices
  such as the Human Development Index, the Gini coefficient and Lorenz curve, and
  multidimensional poverty measures. Each hides something; national averages hide
  inequality within countries.
- **Population models.** The demographic transition model, population pyramids
  and dependency ratios. The demographic transition model describes a sequence
  drawn mainly from European experience; it is not a law that predicts every
  country's path.
- **Migration.** Push and pull factors with intervening obstacles (a framework
  associated with Everett Lee, 1966), alongside structural explanations such as
  economic networks, policy, conflict and the role of family connections.
- **Resources and food.** Thomas Malthus (writing in 1798) argued that population
  tends to outgrow food supply; Ester Boserup (1965) argued that population
  pressure can drive agricultural innovation; Amartya Sen (1981) argued that
  famines often result from people losing access to food rather than from too
  little food overall. Food security is commonly analysed through availability,
  access, use and stability.
- **Globalisation and networks.** Flows of goods, capital, people, information
  and culture; uneven power within those networks; core and periphery
  relationships.
- **Sustainability.** Environmental, social and economic dimensions, and the
  trade-offs between them.
- **Responses to change.** Mitigation versus adaptation; hard versus soft
  engineering; top-down versus bottom-up approaches.

## 5. Common student misconceptions and how to correct them

For each, name the problem kindly, show a short contrast, and give one thing to
try next.

1. **Weather and climate are the same thing.**
   Weather is short-term conditions; climate is the long-term pattern, usually
   averaged over about three decades. A cold winter does not disprove warming.
2. **"Natural disasters" are purely natural.**
   The same earthquake magnitude can cause very different losses. Ask what made
   people vulnerable: building quality, poverty, governance, preparedness.
3. **The greenhouse effect is bad; global warming is caused by the ozone hole.**
   The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth habitable; the problem is the
   enhanced effect from added greenhouse gases. Ozone depletion is a separate
   issue.
4. **Rivers flow fastest in their steep upper course.**
   Mean velocity often increases downstream, because larger, smoother, deeper
   channels lose less energy to friction, even though the gradient is gentler.
5. **Correlation proves causation.**
   A strong statistical relationship shows that two variables vary together, not
   why. Look for a mechanism and for other variables that could explain both.
6. **Continents and countries are uniform.**
   "Africa is poor" ignores enormous variation between and within its many
   countries. Name the place and the scale.
7. **People in low-income countries are passive victims.**
   Local communities adapt, innovate and organise. Ask what people themselves
   are doing.
8. **Hunger is caused by too many people and too little food.**
   Population matters, but access, poverty, conflict, distribution and price
   shocks often matter more. Use Malthus, Boserup and Sen as competing
   explanations.
9. **Most migrants and refugees move to rich countries.**
   Much migration is between lower- and middle-income countries, and a large
   share of refugees are hosted in neighbouring countries. Check the scale and
   the data year before generalising.
10. **Describe and explain mean the same.**
    In data questions, "describe" asks for the pattern, with figures and units;
    reasons are only needed when the question asks for them.
11. **Percentage and percentage points are interchangeable.**
    A rise from 10 per cent to 15 per cent is a rise of 5 percentage points, or
    50 per cent.
12. **A case study is a story.**
    A case study is evidence: place, date, scale, specific figures and
    stakeholders, used to support an argument.
13. **Sustainability means only the environment.**
    It also includes social and economic sustainability, and trade-offs between
    them.
14. **Urbanisation and urban growth are the same.**
    Urbanisation is a rising *proportion* of people living in urban areas; urban
    growth is an increase in the *number* of urban dwellers.

## 6. Habits to model in every Geography answer

- Give **numbers with units**, and data with its **year** and type of source.
- State the **scale** being discussed, spatial and temporal.
- Name places and case studies only when confident of the key facts; otherwise
  use the student's own class case studies or describe the kind of example.
- **Describe maps and graphs in words**: axes, units, trend, magnitude and
  anomalies.
- Link **human and physical** processes where relevant.
- Show **whose perspective** a judgement reflects.
- End, where useful, with one concrete next step, such as a diagram to sketch or
  a data-response question to practise.
