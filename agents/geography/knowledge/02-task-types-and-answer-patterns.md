# Geography: task types and answer patterns

This document describes the Geography tasks IB students bring, how to approach
each, and how to give provisional, criterion-based feedback. It is deliberately
generic: paper structures, mark totals and criteria change between editions of
the IB subject guide, so course-specific points are marked for verification.
Where the specialist's own knowledge base describes the current course, follow
it.

---

## 1. How command terms set depth

These plain-word descriptions were written for this supplement; they are not the
IB's official definitions.

| Command term | What it asks for, in plain words | In Geography, a strong response… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| State, identify, define | A short, specific answer or meaning | Is precise, with a unit or place where relevant |
| Outline | The main points, briefly | Lists key points in a logical order |
| Describe | What something is like or what the data shows | Gives the overall pattern, specific values with units, and any anomaly |
| Calculate, estimate | Work out a value, or an approximate one | Shows working and gives the unit |
| Draw, label, annotate | Produce or add to a diagram or map | Annotations explain, not just name (in text-only help, describe what to draw) |
| Suggest | Offer plausible reasons, often for unfamiliar data | Gives reasoned possibilities, hedged appropriately |
| Distinguish | Make clear the difference between two things | States the difference directly, with an example |
| Explain | Show why or how something happens | Gives the mechanism, step by step |
| Analyse, examine | Break an issue into parts and show how they connect | Shows relationships, such as human and physical, or cause and effect |
| Compare, contrast | Show similarities, differences or both | Works point by point with data for each place |
| Evaluate | Weigh strengths against weaknesses and reach a verdict | Judges effectiveness for whom, at what scale and over what time |
| Discuss | Explore several sides, then conclude | Balanced, evidenced, with a clear final position |
| To what extent | Measure how far a claim holds | States a degree and supports it with case-study evidence |
| Justify | Back a choice or conclusion with reasons | Gives grounds and evidence |

The same topic at different depths:
- *"Define lag time."* The time between peak rainfall and peak discharge.
- *"Explain why urban catchments often have short lag times."* Mechanisms:
  impermeable surfaces, drains, reduced infiltration and interception.
- *"Evaluate the effectiveness of strategies to reduce urban flood risk."*
  Judgement across strategies, stakeholders, costs and time scales.

## 2. Task types and how to approach them

### 2.1 Short-answer and data-response questions
Students meet maps, graphs, tables, photographs and diagrams, then answer short
"describe", "calculate", "suggest" or "explain" questions.

- **The specialist cannot see figures in the text-only pilot.** If the student
  refers to "Figure 2" without the data, ask for the values or a description of
  axes, units and key points. Never guess what a figure shows.
- **Describing data:** overall trend or pattern first; then specific values with
  units and dates; then rate of change or variation; then any anomaly. Many
  teachers use a short acronym for this; use the student's if they have one.
- **Calculations:** show the formula, substitution, result and unit, rounded
  sensibly.
- **"Suggest" questions:** give two or three plausible, distinct reasons linked
  to the context shown, and hedge ("this could be because…").
- Match length to the marks or command term the student reports; short-answer
  marks usually reward specific, separate points.

### 2.2 Structured explanation questions
Explain a process or pattern, often with a diagram.
- Lead with the core mechanism in one sentence, then develop it step by step.
- Use correct terminology (for example, "throughflow", "longshore drift",
  "natural increase").
- Describe in words what a diagram should show and how to annotate it, since the
  specialist cannot draw: axes, labels, arrows and the explanatory annotations.

### 2.3 Extended responses and essays
In the current course, extended responses appear in option themes, in the core
and, at HL, in an extension on global interactions (verify the current structure
against the current IB subject guide).

Strong extended responses:
- **Answer the question directly** in the introduction, defining key terms and
  scope.
- **Use detailed, located evidence**: named places, dates, figures, at more than
  one scale where relevant.
- **Explain processes** rather than listing facts.
- **Consider perspectives**: different stakeholders, places or groups.
- **Weigh and conclude**: a judgement that follows from the evidence, often
  noting how the answer varies by place, scale or time.

The specialist helps by unpacking the question, building a plan, suggesting what
kinds of evidence fit, and testing the student's case studies. For graded work,
it gives feedback rather than writing.

### 2.4 The fieldwork internal assessment
The internal assessment in Geography is based on fieldwork. In the current
course it is understood to require primary data collected by the student, a
written report within a word limit, and criteria covering the question, methods,
data treatment, analysis, conclusion and evaluation (verify the current
requirements, word limit and criteria against the current IB subject guide).

**The specialist may:**
- help the student sharpen their own fieldwork question or hypothesis;
- explain sampling strategies (random, systematic, stratified) and their
  trade-offs;
- explain presentation techniques and statistical methods, demonstrated on
  made-up data, not the student's;
- ask questions that help the student interpret their own results;
- give feedback on drafts the student has written, with provisional marks where
  appropriate.

**The specialist must not:**
- write any section of the report;
- process the student's data into findings or conclusions for them;
- invent data, locations or secondary sources.

**Common pitfalls:** a question that cannot be measured; too few sample points
for the method; graphs chosen for appearance rather than fit; analysis that
describes without explaining; conclusions that go beyond the data; and
evaluations that list generic problems ("it rained") without saying how they
affected the results.

### 2.5 The extended essay in Geography
The student owns the question, data and writing. The specialist can help narrow
the question, discuss methods and evidence, and respond to drafts. Extended essay
criteria differ from course criteria (verify against the current extended essay
guide).

### 2.6 Revision and exam practice
- **Case-study cards:** place, date, scale, three or four key figures with
  units, stakeholders, and one evaluative point.
- **Diagram drills:** describe and annotate key diagrams from memory.
- **Data practice:** original practice data sets, clearly labelled as made up.
- **Essay planning:** quick plans for original practice questions.

## 3. What strong Geography answers have in common

- The depth matches the command term.
- Numbers have units, dates and a sense of source type.
- Places are named and scaled, and generalisations are avoided.
- Human and physical processes are linked where relevant.
- Perspectives and power are considered in evaluative answers.
- Uncertainty is admitted honestly, especially for current figures.
- It is complete, and ends with a useful next step where that helps.

## 4. Giving provisional, criterion-based feedback

### 4.1 Before marking
1. Get the **exact question**, its command term and the marks available, as the
   student reports them.
2. Get the **criteria or markscheme** the student's teacher uses, or use those in
   the specialist's own knowledge base. If neither is available, ask or give
   qualitative feedback without a number.
3. Be clear about **what part of the work** the judgement covers.

### 4.2 Reading the work
1. Does it answer the question at the right depth?
2. Is the evidence specific, located and accurate, with units?
3. Are processes explained, not just named?
4. Are scale and perspectives considered?
5. Is there a supported judgement where the command term needs one?

### 4.3 Writing the feedback
- **What works**, pointing to the student's words.
- **What would move it up**, two or three actions in order of impact.
- **Provisional judgement**, if criteria and scope allow, with the word
  "provisional" in the same sentence as any mark, band or level, and a reminder
  that only the teacher and the IB award real marks.
- **Next step**, one task to do now.

Do not rewrite the student's work. Demonstrate a technique on a different
example instead.

### 4.4 An illustrative feedback rubric

This rubric is **illustrative, not the official IB criteria**. Use it only for
qualitative feedback when no actual criteria are available, and do not convert
it into a numerical IB mark.

| Dimension | Developing | Secure | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Focus on the question | Partly relevant | Addresses the question throughout | Addresses all parts with a clear line of argument |
| Evidence and place detail | General, unlocated | Named places with some figures | Precise, located, multi-scale evidence with units |
| Explanation of processes | Processes named | Processes explained | Processes linked across human and physical systems |
| Evaluation and perspectives | Single viewpoint, no judgement | Some weighing | Balanced weighing with a justified, qualified conclusion |

### 4.5 Expressing a provisional mark
- Use the scale the student or the criteria supply, and prefer a range when
  judging part of an answer.
- Example wording: "Provisional estimate: around 4 out of 10 if the rest of the
  answer matched this paragraph; only your teacher and the IB award real marks."
- Never inflate a mark and never present it as what the IB "would" award.
