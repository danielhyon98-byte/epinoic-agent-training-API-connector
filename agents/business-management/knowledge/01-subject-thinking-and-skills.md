# Business Management: subject thinking and skills

This document adds to your own Business Management knowledge base. It does not
replace it. If anything here disagrees with your master prompt or knowledge base,
follow those and treat this text as wrong.

## 1. How a business-management thinker approaches a problem

Business Management looks at decisions **from inside an organisation**: what should
this business do, given its objectives, resources, stakeholders and environment?
(Economics looks at whole markets and economies; see the scope section of the
answering document.) Good answers rest on these habits:

- **Context first.** Every answer is about *this* organisation: its size, legal
  form, objectives, finances, people and market. Generic textbook points score
  poorly if they are not applied.
- **Stakeholders.** Owners, employees, managers, customers, suppliers, lenders,
  government and the local community want different things. A decision that helps
  one group often costs another.
- **Internal and external.** Strengths and weaknesses are inside the business.
  Opportunities and threats come from outside.
- **Numbers and judgement together.** A calculation tells you what *is* under its
  assumptions. Qualitative factors (morale, brand, ethics) decide what *should*
  happen.
- **Short term and long term.** Cutting training raises profit this year and may
  reduce productivity later.
- **Uncertainty.** Forecasts, market research and break-even charts rest on
  assumptions that may not hold. Say which ones matter.

The course is framed around a small set of key concepts, believed to be change,
creativity, ethics and sustainability (verify against the current IB subject guide).
Use them to deepen analysis, not as labels.

## 2. Content areas, in brief

Your own knowledge base holds the syllabus detail. Students typically ask about:

- **Introduction to business:** types of organisation, objectives, stakeholders,
  growth and its methods, multinational companies.
- **Human resource management:** workforce planning, organisational structure,
  leadership styles, motivation theories, culture, communication, employer and
  employee relations.
- **Finance and accounts:** sources of finance, costs and revenues, final accounts,
  ratio analysis, cash flow, investment appraisal, budgets.
- **Marketing:** market research, segmentation and positioning, the marketing mix,
  branding, the product life cycle, international marketing.
- **Operations management:** production methods, lean production and quality,
  location, break-even, supply chains, research and development, crisis management.

Some topics and calculations are taught only at HL (verify against the current IB
subject guide). Do not tell a student something is "HL only" unless your knowledge
base says so.

## 3. The business toolkit, in our own words

| Tool | What it is for | Using it well | Common error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SWOT | Auditing the current position of one organisation | Few, specific, prioritised points tied to the case; then say which matter most and what follows | Long generic lists; putting external factors under strengths |
| STEEPLE | Scanning the external environment (social, technological, economic, environmental, political, legal, ethical) | Pick the factors that actually affect this business and explain the impact | Covering all seven letters thinly |
| Ansoff matrix | Comparing growth options by product (existing or new) and market (existing or new) | Discuss risk relative to this firm's capabilities | Saying diversification is always too risky |
| Boston matrix | Reviewing a product portfolio by market growth and relative market share | Link to cash flow: which products fund which | Treating the four labels as permanent |
| Decision tree | Comparing options under uncertainty using probabilities and expected values | Show expected value working; then question the probabilities | Forgetting to subtract the cost of each option |
| Force-field analysis | Weighing driving forces against restraining forces for a change | Score the forces and explain how restraining forces could be reduced | Treating the scores as objective |
| Break-even analysis | Finding the output where total revenue equals total costs | Use contribution; add margin of safety; state the assumptions | Dividing fixed costs by price instead of contribution |
| Ratio analysis | Judging profitability, liquidity, efficiency and gearing | Compare over time or with competitors; explain causes | Calculating ratios without interpreting them |
| Investment appraisal | Comparing projects by payback, average rate of return and net present value | Use more than one method and add qualitative factors | Ignoring the timing of cash flows |
| Gantt chart and critical path | Planning project tasks and identifying the activities that cannot be delayed | Explain what happens if a critical activity slips | Confusing float with duration |

Which of these tools form part of the course's toolkit, and at which level, should be
checked (verify against the current IB subject guide).

## 4. Key formulas

State the formula in words or symbols before substituting. Where the student's
formula sheet uses a different version, follow theirs.

| Measure | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Contribution per unit | price − variable cost per unit |
| Break-even quantity | fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit (round **up** to whole units) |
| Margin of safety | actual or forecast output − break-even quantity |
| Output for a target profit | (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit |
| Profit | total contribution − fixed costs, or total revenue − total costs |
| Gross profit margin | gross profit ÷ sales revenue × 100 |
| Profit margin | profit before interest and tax ÷ sales revenue × 100 |
| Return on capital employed | profit before interest and tax ÷ capital employed × 100 |
| Current ratio | current assets ÷ current liabilities |
| Acid-test ratio | (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities |
| Payback period | time until cumulative net cash flow equals the initial cost |
| Average rate of return | ((total net returns − capital cost) ÷ years of use) ÷ capital cost × 100 |
| Net present value | sum of discounted net cash flows − initial cost |
| Straight-line depreciation | (cost − residual value) ÷ years of useful life |
| Mark-up | (price − cost) ÷ cost × 100 |

The definitions of capital employed, and of the efficiency and gearing ratios, vary
slightly between textbooks. Follow the student's formula sheet (verify against the current IB subject guide).

### A worked investment appraisal (hypothetical figures)

A firm considers a machine costing **$50,000**. Expected net cash flows: Year 1
$15,000, Year 2 $20,000, Year 3 $20,000, Year 4 $10,000. Discount factors at 10%,
as a question might supply them: 0.909, 0.826, 0.751, 0.683.

- **Payback:** cumulative cash flow is $15,000 after Year 1 and $35,000 after Year 2.
  $15,000 more is needed out of Year 3's $20,000: 15,000 ÷ 20,000 = 0.75 of a year
  = 9 months. Payback = **2 years 9 months**.
- **Average rate of return:** total net returns $65,000 − cost $50,000 = $15,000 over
  4 years = $3,750 a year. $3,750 ÷ $50,000 × 100 = **7.5%**.
- **Net present value:** 15,000 × 0.909 = 13,635; 20,000 × 0.826 = 16,520;
  20,000 × 0.751 = 15,020; 10,000 × 0.683 = 6,830. Total present value = $52,005.
  NPV = $52,005 − $50,000 = **$2,005**.
- **Interpretation:** the NPV is positive, so at a 10% discount rate the project
  earns more than its cost. But the margin is small, so a modest fall in forecast
  cash flows would make it negative. An average rate of return of 7.5% should be
  compared with the cost of borrowing.

## 5. Analytical and evaluative skills

- **Apply:** use names, figures and facts from the case, not from your general
  knowledge of real firms.
- **Analyse:** explain *why* a figure changed or *how* a decision would affect
  stakeholders, step by step.
- **Evaluate:** weigh advantages against disadvantages for this business. Useful
  angles are short term and long term, stakeholder conflict, how reliable the data
  and forecasts are, the assumptions behind a tool, ethical and sustainability
  effects, and what else the firm could do.
- **Recommend:** choose an option, give the deciding reason, and say what would
  change the decision ("Choose option A, provided the sales forecast is at least
  2,000 units").

## 6. Common misconceptions and how to correct them

| Misconception | Correction to give |
| --- | --- |
| "Profitable firms can't run out of cash." | Profit is revenue minus costs over a period. Cash is money available now. Credit sales and big purchases can leave a profitable firm illiquid. |
| "Revenue and profit are the same." | Revenue is price × quantity sold. Profit subtracts costs. |
| "Break-even = fixed costs ÷ price." | Divide by contribution per unit (price − variable cost), because each sale must also cover its own variable cost. |
| "A 33% mark-up means a 33% margin." | Cost $60, price $80: mark-up = 20 ÷ 60 = 33.3% of cost; margin = 20 ÷ 80 = 25% of price. |
| "Profit margin rose from 12% to 15%, a 3% rise." | It rose by 3 percentage points, which is a 25% relative increase. |
| "A higher current ratio is always better." | Very high liquidity can mean idle cash or excess inventory. Judge it against the firm's needs. |
| "Depreciation is cash leaving the business." | It spreads the cost of an asset over its life in the accounts. No cash moves when it is recorded. |
| "Payback is the best method because it is simple." | It ignores cash flows after payback and the time value of money. Use it alongside ARR or NPV. |
| "Pay is the best motivator." | Content theories such as Herzberg's suggest pay mainly prevents dissatisfaction, while recognition, responsibility and the work itself motivate. Evidence varies by context. |
| "SWOT is a decision." | SWOT describes a position. The decision comes from what you do with it. |
