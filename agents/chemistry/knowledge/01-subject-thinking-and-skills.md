# Chemistry: subject thinking and skills

This document describes how a strong Chemistry tutor thinks and which habits it
should model for an IB Diploma student. It does not list the syllabus. The IB
sciences guides were recently revised, so do not quote topic numbers, theme names
or SL/HL assignments as fact unless the student supplies them or your own knowledge
base confirms them.

## 1. Core ideas that recur across the course

A thinking map, not an official topic list:

- **The mole and conservation of mass.** Atoms are rearranged, not created or
  destroyed. Amount of substance links the particle scale to measurable masses,
  volumes and concentrations.
- **Structure determines properties.** Ionic, covalent, metallic and molecular
  structures explain melting points, conductivity, solubility and hardness. The
  strength of intermolecular forces explains volatility in molecular substances.
- **Energy changes.** Bond breaking absorbs energy; bond forming releases it. The
  balance gives the enthalpy change. Entropy and Gibbs energy decide spontaneity
  where the course includes them.
- **Rates.** Reactions need collisions with enough energy and suitable orientation.
  Temperature, concentration, surface area and catalysts change the rate.
- **Equilibrium.** Many reactions are reversible and reach a dynamic equilibrium
  described by an equilibrium constant that depends on temperature.
- **Proton and electron transfer.** Acid–base reactions transfer protons; redox
  reactions transfer electrons. Oxidation states keep track of electron transfer.
- **Organic chemistry.** Functional groups determine reactions; mechanisms explain
  them; naming follows systematic rules.
- **Analysis.** Spectra and titration curves are evidence about structure and
  amount.

## 2. Thinking like a chemist

### Three levels of description

Strong explanations move deliberately between what is observed (macroscopic), what
particles are doing (sub-microscopic) and how it is written (symbolic). "The
solution turned colourless" is an observation; "bromine molecules added across the
C=C double bond" is a particle explanation; the equation is the symbolic record. Ask
students which level they are writing at when an explanation is muddled.

### Models and their limits

- Lewis structures, VSEPR, the ideal gas, the Bohr-style shell model and oxidation
  states are models. Each predicts some things well and fails elsewhere: the shell
  model does not explain sub-levels; oxidation states are bookkeeping, not real
  charges on covalent atoms.
- When a model fails, name the better one: for example, delocalised electrons
  explain the equal bond lengths in benzene, which a single alternating structure
  does not.

### Evidence

- Spectra (infrared, mass and, where taught, NMR) identify bonds, molecular mass and
  environments of atoms. Combine evidence from several techniques before concluding.
- Titration and calorimetry give quantitative evidence. Their reliability depends on
  the method as much as the arithmetic.

### Uncertainty and error

- **Measurement uncertainty** comes from the apparatus and how it is read. A titre
  is the difference of two burette readings, so its absolute uncertainty is the sum
  of the two reading uncertainties.
- **Percentage error** compares the result with an accepted value. If the
  percentage error is larger than the total percentage uncertainty, random error
  alone cannot explain the difference, so look for a systematic error.
- Worked illustration (numbers invented for teaching): a neutralisation experiment
  gives ΔH = −54 kJ mol⁻¹ with a temperature rise of 6.5 K, each thermometer reading
  ±0.1 K.
  Uncertainty in ΔT = 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.2 K
  Percentage uncertainty in ΔT = (0.2 / 6.5) × 100 = 3.08 %, about 3.1 %
  Taking a commonly quoted value of about −57 kJ mol⁻¹:
  percentage error = (|−54 − (−57)| / 57) × 100 = 5.26 %, about 5.3 %
  The error exceeds the uncertainty, which points to a systematic effect such as
  heat loss to the surroundings. Heat loss makes the measured value less
  exothermic, which matches the direction of the error.
- **Significant figures.** Give the final answer to the number of significant
  figures of the least precise data. For pH, the number of decimal places matches
  the significant figures of the concentration: [H⁺] = 0.010 mol dm⁻³ gives
  pH = 2.00.

### Variables and controls

In a rate experiment, name the independent variable (for example, concentration of
acid), the dependent variable and how it is measured (time for a fixed volume of gas,
or mass loss), and the control variables with **how** each is kept constant
(temperature in a water bath, same mass and particle size of solid, same total
volume). A control variable listed without a method or reason earns little.

## 3. Common misconceptions and how to correct them

Find out what the student believes, give a case the wrong idea gets wrong, teach the
correct model and finish with one check question.

| Misconception | Correct idea to teach | Check question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breaking bonds releases energy. | Breaking bonds always absorbs energy. Energy is released when new bonds form. | Why is the combustion of methane exothermic if it breaks C–H bonds? |
| Boiling water breaks O–H bonds. | Boiling a molecular substance overcomes intermolecular forces; covalent bonds inside molecules stay intact. | What is present in the steam above boiling water? |
| A hydrogen bond is the O–H bond in water. | A hydrogen bond is an intermolecular attraction between an H atom bonded to N, O or F and a lone pair on N, O or F in another molecule. | How many molecules are involved in one hydrogen bond? |
| Sodium chloride is made of NaCl molecules. | It is a lattice of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions; the formula gives the ratio of ions. | Why does solid NaCl not conduct but molten NaCl does? |
| At equilibrium, reactant and product concentrations are equal and the reaction has stopped. | The forward and reverse rates are equal; concentrations are constant, not necessarily equal. | What would happen to the isotopic label if a labelled product were added to an equilibrium mixture? |
| A catalyst increases the yield at equilibrium. | A catalyst speeds up forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached faster with the same position and the same K. | Does a catalyst change the value of K? |
| Adding more reactant changes K. | For a given reaction, K changes only with temperature. Adding reactant shifts the position; K is unchanged. | What happens to K if the system is heated, for an exothermic forward reaction? |
| A strong acid is a concentrated acid. | Strength is the extent of ionisation; concentration is the amount per volume. A dilute strong acid and a concentrated weak acid both exist. | Is 0.010 mol dm⁻³ HCl strong or concentrated? |
| pH is linear. | pH is logarithmic: a tenfold dilution of a strong acid raises pH by 1. | What is the pH of 0.0010 mol dm⁻³ HCl? |
| Higher temperature speeds reactions only because particles collide more often. | The main effect is that a much larger fraction of collisions have energy at least equal to the activation energy. | Use a Maxwell–Boltzmann sketch to show what changes when temperature rises. |
| Atoms "want" a full outer shell. | Explain with energy and electrostatics: the arrangement formed has lower energy because of attraction between nuclei and electrons. | Why does sodium need energy input to form Na⁺(g) from Na(g)? |
| Mass is lost when a gas is produced. | Mass is conserved; the gas leaves an open container. | How would you show conservation of mass for a reaction producing CO₂? |
| Oxidation means gaining oxygen. | Oxidation is the loss of electrons, or an increase in oxidation state. | Is Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻ an oxidation? |
| Electrons flow through the salt bridge. | Electrons flow through the external wire; ions move through the salt bridge to keep each half-cell neutral. | Which way do anions move in the salt bridge of a working cell? |

## 4. Skills the specialist should model

- **Amount calculations** with the relation written first: n = m / M; n = cV;
  pV = nRT. Name each quantity and unit.
- **Unit conversions on their own line:** 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³; 1 m³ = 1000 dm³;
  1 kPa = 1000 Pa; 1 kJ = 1000 J; temperature in K = temperature in °C + 273.15
  (many IB materials round this to 273; use what your data booklet uses).
- **Balanced equations with state symbols**, followed by an atom check and a charge
  check. Example: C₃H₈(g) + 5O₂(g) → 3CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(l); C: 3 = 3; H: 8 = 8;
  O: 10 = 6 + 4.
- **Sign conventions:** an exothermic change has a negative ΔH. State the sign
  explicitly in every enthalpy answer.
- **Naming and formulas** in text form: condensed structural formulas such as
  CH₃CH₂OH, with the systematic name. Skeletal formulas cannot be drawn in plain
  text, so describe them in words if needed.
- **Sense checks:** a concentration of 85 mol dm⁻³ or a molar mass below 1 g mol⁻¹
  signals an error.
