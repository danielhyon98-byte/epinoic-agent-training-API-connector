# Chemistry: task types and answer patterns

This document covers the kinds of request a Chemistry student brings and the answer
pattern that works for each. It does not describe the official paper structure,
timings or mark totals. If those matter, tell the student to check the current
subject guide or their teacher.

## 1. Multiple-choice reasoning

**Approach**

1. Identify the chemistry being tested before reading the options.
2. Predict the answer or its form.
3. Eliminate with quick tests: conservation of atoms and charge, units, sign of ΔH,
   oxidation-state changes, and whether the option even describes the right kind of
   substance (ions versus molecules).
4. Explain why the correct option is right and why each distractor fails.

**Example of a charge check.** Which equation is balanced?
(A) Cu(s) + Ag⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + Ag(s)
(B) Cu(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s)
Both have the same atoms on each side, but in (A) the charge is +1 on the left and
+2 on the right. In (B) the charge is +2 on both sides, so (B) is balanced.

Whether a calculator or data booklet is allowed in a multiple-choice component is a
course-structure fact; do not state it unless your own knowledge base confirms it
(verify against the current IB subject guide).

## 2. Short and structured answers

- Read the command term and the mark allocation together. Marks suggest how many
  distinct chemical points are expected.
- One point per idea, applied to the substances in the question. "It has strong
  bonds" is vague; "a giant covalent lattice in which every atom is joined by strong
  covalent bonds, so much energy is needed to break them" is precise.
- Name the particles and forces involved: ions, molecules, delocalised electrons,
  London (dispersion) forces, dipole–dipole forces, hydrogen bonds.
- Equations count as chemistry: include state symbols unless told otherwise.

## 3. Data-based questions

**Approach**

1. Read axes, units and scales, or the table headings.
2. Describe the trend in words, then quantify it.
3. Interpret with chemistry: link gradients to rates, plateaus to completion,
   steep sections of titration curves to equivalence, absorptions to bonds, and
   m/z peaks to fragments or the molecular ion.
4. Comment on anomalies and reliability.

**Common data types**

- **Rate graphs.** Initial rate is the gradient of a tangent at t = 0, with units
  such as mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ or cm³ s⁻¹.
- **Titration curves.** The equivalence point is in the steepest section; an
  indicator is suitable when its colour change happens within that steep range.
- **Infrared spectra.** Absorptions are compared with the ranges in the student's
  data booklet. A broad absorption at high wavenumber suggests O–H; a strong
  absorption around 1700 cm⁻¹ suggests C=O. Always combine with other evidence.
- **Mass spectra.** The peak at the highest m/z (other than small isotope peaks) is
  usually the molecular ion; differences between peaks suggest lost fragments.

Never invent spectra, peak positions or readings. If a student mentions data they
have not pasted, explain the method and ask for the values.

## 4. Calculations

**Pattern (use every time)**

1. Balanced equation with state symbols, followed by an atom check.
2. Givens with units, converted on their own lines (cm³ to dm³).
3. Relation in symbols, then explicit substitution.
4. Mole ratio from the equation, stated as a ratio.
5. Result with unit, sign where relevant, and significant figures.
6. Sense check.

**Example (numbers invented for teaching).** 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl(aq) is
mixed with 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH(aq). The temperature rises by 6.5 K.
Find the enthalpy change of neutralisation. Assume the solution has density
1.00 g cm⁻³ and specific heat capacity 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.

HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
Atom check: H 2 = 2; Cl 1 = 1; Na 1 = 1; O 1 = 1

m = (50.0 + 50.0) cm³ × 1.00 g cm⁻³ = 100.0 g
q = mcΔT = (100.0)(4.18)(6.5) = 2717 J (unrounded)
V(HCl) = 50.0 cm³ = 0.0500 dm³
n(HCl) = cV = (1.00)(0.0500) = 0.0500 mol
Ratio HCl : H₂O = 1 : 1, so n(H₂O) = 0.0500 mol
ΔH = −q / n = −(2717) / (0.0500) = −5.434 × 10⁴ J mol⁻¹
ΔH = −54 kJ mol⁻¹ (2 s.f., limited by ΔT)

The sign is negative because the reaction released energy, which raised the
temperature of the solution.

## 5. Extended responses ("explain", "discuss", "evaluate")

- State the key claim, then the particle-level reasoning in short linked steps.
- Use energy and electrostatic language rather than "wanting".
- For "evaluate" or "discuss", weigh at least two factors (for example, yield
  against rate in choosing conditions for an industrial equilibrium) and reach a
  judgement.

**Example chain.** Why does first ionisation energy generally increase across a
period? Nuclear charge increases by one each step → electrons are added to the same
main energy level → shielding by inner electrons stays similar → the outer electron
is held more strongly → more energy is needed to remove it. Then mention that the
exceptions (such as the dips at the start of a new sub-level or where electrons
first pair in an orbital) show the sub-level model at work.

## 6. Practical work and internal assessment guidance

Help the student plan, check and improve their own work; never write text for
submission (see this specialist's answering-functionality document).

- A focused research question names one independent variable, one dependent
  variable and the chemical system.
- Each control variable needs a method and a reason.
- Choose a range and enough values to show a trend; repeat where readings vary.
- Show one sample calculation; propagate uncertainties from the apparatus used.
- In evaluation, distinguish random from systematic effects and propose specific
  improvements (for example, insulating the calorimeter and extrapolating the
  cooling curve).
- Safety: hazards of the actual chemicals, eye protection, ventilation, disposal.
  Tell the student to follow their school's risk assessment.

Official IA criterion names, weightings and length limits are not stated here.
Verify them against the current IB subject guide.

## 7. Feedback on drafts and answers

- Point to the student's own words, equation or line of working.
- Fix chemistry errors first (wrong particles, wrong forces, unbalanced equations,
  sign errors), then communication.
- Give two or three priorities with the specific change for each.
- Any mark is labelled provisional in the same sentence.

## 8. Command-term depth

Paraphrases for tutoring; the official definitions are in the current subject guide.

| Command term | What the answer must do |
| --- | --- |
| State, identify | Give a specific name, value, formula or fact without explanation. |
| Outline | Give the main points briefly. |
| Describe | Give a detailed account of observations, features or a trend. |
| Explain | Give the reasons or mechanism at particle level. |
| Calculate, determine | Reach a numerical answer with working and units. |
| Deduce | Reach a conclusion, formula, equation or structure from the information given. |
| Formulate (an equation) | Write a balanced equation, with state symbols unless told otherwise. |
| Draw, sketch | Produce a structure, diagram or graph with key features labelled. In text, describe it clearly. |
| Predict | Give an expected result with a brief reason. |
| Suggest | Propose a plausible answer where more than one may be valid. |
| Compare and contrast | Give similarities and differences, referring to both throughout. |
| Discuss, evaluate | Weigh strengths and limitations and reach a judgement. |
| Justify | Support a choice or conclusion with evidence or reasoning. |

## 9. Provisional criterion-based feedback

**Exam-style questions with a stated mark allocation:** build illustrative mark
points from the chemistry, say which the student earned and what is missing, and
label the number provisional in the same sentence, for example: "Provisional mark:
2 out of 3 — only your teacher and the IB award real marks." With no allocation,
give qualitative feedback or ask for it.

**Investigation drafts:** when the student has not supplied the official criteria,
use this rubric. It is illustrative, not the official IB criteria.

| Aspect (illustrative) | Developing | Secure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | Broad question; system or variables unclear. | Defined system with one independent and one dependent variable. |
| Method | Controls listed without method; no safety detail. | Each control has a method and reason; specific hazards and disposal addressed. |
| Data and processing | Few values, no uncertainties, unclear calculation. | Raw data with uncertainties, a clear sample calculation and propagated uncertainty. |
| Answering the question | Restates numbers. | Answers the question with the processed data and compares with chemical theory or an accepted value, considering uncertainty. |
| Limitations and improvements | Generic weaknesses. | Specific systematic and random effects with targeted improvements. |

Give band-style comments rather than a number unless the student supplies the
official criteria; any number is then provisional, labelled in the same sentence.
