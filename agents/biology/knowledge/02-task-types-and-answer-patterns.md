# Biology: task types and answer patterns

This document covers the kinds of request a Biology student brings and the answer
pattern that works for each. It does not describe the official paper structure,
timings or mark totals. If those matter, tell the student to check the current
subject guide or their teacher.

## 1. Multiple-choice reasoning

**Approach**

1. Decide what biological idea is being tested before reading the options.
2. Read every option; many distractors are true statements that do not answer the
   question asked.
3. Eliminate with knowledge of exceptions, levels of organisation and precise
   vocabulary (for example, "diffusion" versus "osmosis", "gene" versus "allele").
4. Explain why the correct option is right and why each distractor fails.

**Example.** Which structure is found in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells:
nucleus, mitochondrion, ribosome or endoplasmic reticulum? Prokaryotes have no
membrane-bound organelles, which rules out the nucleus, mitochondrion and endoplasmic
reticulum. Ribosomes are found in both (smaller in prokaryotes).

## 2. Short and structured answers

- Read the command term and the mark allocation together. Marks suggest how many
  distinct biological points are expected.
- Each point should be specific and linked to the context. "The enzyme stops
  working" is vague; "the active site changes shape, so the substrate can no longer
  bind" is precise.
- Use the correct level: if the question asks about the organism, finish at the
  organism, even if the mechanism starts in the cell.

## 3. Data-based questions

Biology data questions often use unfamiliar organisms or experiments. The skill is
applying known principles to new data.

**Approach**

1. Read the introduction: what was varied, what was measured, how many samples.
2. Read axes, units and scales or table headings.
3. Describe the trend with values and units from the data, including any change in
   the trend.
4. Calculate when it helps comparison: percentage change, rate, mean, difference.
5. Explain using biology, and say when the explanation is a suggestion rather than
   something the data show.
6. Evaluate: sample size, replicates, controls, spread of data, whether a
   correlation could have another cause.

**Example (invented data).** A potato cylinder goes from 2.50 g to 2.20 g in a sucrose
solution.
Percentage change = (final − initial) / initial × 100 = (2.20 − 2.50) / 2.50 × 100 = −12.0 %
Interpretation: the cylinder lost water by osmosis, so the solution had a higher
solute concentration than the potato cells.

Never invent data. If a student refers to a graph or table they have not pasted,
explain the method and ask for the values.

## 4. Calculations

**Pattern (use every time)**

1. Givens with units; convert to the same unit on its own line.
2. Relation in words or symbols.
3. Explicit substitution.
4. Result with unit (or "no unit" for magnification and ratios) and a sensible
   number of significant figures.
5. Sense check against known sizes or rates.

**Common calculations:** magnification and actual size, percentage change, rates
(for example, oxygen produced: 3.6 cm³ in 12 min gives 3.6 / 12 = 0.30 cm³ min⁻¹),
means and ranges, ratios in genetic crosses, and simple index calculations where the
course uses them.

## 5. Extended responses ("explain", "discuss", "evaluate")

- Plan the points before writing; each paragraph makes one idea and links it to
  the question.
- Connect structure to function and mechanism to outcome.
- Use specific examples only when confident of them.
- For "evaluate" or "discuss", give evidence on more than one side and reach a
  supported judgement.

**Example chain.** How are alveoli adapted for gas exchange? Very many alveoli → a
large total surface area for diffusion; walls one cell thick, close to capillaries
→ short diffusion distance; ventilation and continuous blood flow → steep
concentration gradients maintained; moist lining → gases dissolve before diffusing.

## 6. Practical work and internal assessment guidance

Help the student plan, check and improve their own investigation; never write text
for submission (see this specialist's answering-functionality document).

- A focused research question names the independent variable, the dependent
  variable and the organism or system.
- Each control variable needs a method and a reason; include a control treatment
  where it helps attribute the effect.
- Plan enough replicates for biological variation, and a range of values that shows
  a trend.
- Choose a measure of spread and, where appropriate, a statistical test; explain
  what the test can and cannot show.
- Ethics: minimise harm to organisms, avoid procedures that cause distress, obtain
  consent for human participants, and follow the school's and the IB's guidance.
- Safety: microorganisms, chemicals and sharp tools under supervision.

Official IA criterion names, weightings and length limits are not stated here.
Verify them against the current IB subject guide.

## 7. Feedback on drafts and answers

- Point to the student's words or line of working.
- Fix biological errors first (wrong level, wrong process, anthropomorphic
  language such as "the cell wants"), then communication.
- Give two or three priorities, each with the specific change.
- Any mark is labelled provisional in the same sentence.

## 8. Command-term depth

Paraphrases for tutoring; the official definitions are in the current subject guide.

| Command term | What the answer must do |
| --- | --- |
| State, identify, label | Give a specific name, fact or label without explanation. |
| Outline | Give the main points briefly. |
| Describe | Give a detailed account of a structure, process or trend, without causes. |
| Annotate | Add brief explanatory notes to a diagram or graph. |
| Distinguish | Give the differences between two things. |
| Explain | Give reasons or mechanisms: why or how. |
| Calculate, determine | Reach a numerical answer with working and units. |
| Deduce | Reach a conclusion that follows from the information given. |
| Predict | Give an expected outcome with a brief reason. |
| Suggest | Propose a plausible explanation where more than one may be valid. |
| Compare and contrast | Give similarities and differences, referring to both throughout. |
| Discuss, evaluate | Weigh evidence, strengths and limitations, and reach a judgement. |
| Justify | Support a choice or conclusion with evidence or reasoning. |

## 9. Provisional criterion-based feedback

**Exam-style questions with a stated mark allocation:** build illustrative mark
points from the biology, say which the student earned and what is missing, and label
the number provisional in the same sentence, for example: "Provisional mark: 2 out
of 4 — only your teacher and the IB award real marks." With no allocation, give
qualitative feedback or ask for it.

**Investigation drafts:** when the student has not supplied the official criteria,
use this rubric. It is illustrative, not the official IB criteria.

| Aspect (illustrative) | Developing | Secure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | Broad question; organism or variables unclear. | Defined organism or system, one independent and one dependent variable. |
| Method | Few replicates; controls listed without method; ethics not considered. | Adequate replicates, controls with methods and reasons, ethical and safety issues addressed. |
| Data and processing | Raw numbers only; no spread shown. | Processed data with means and spread, a clear sample calculation and an appropriate graph. |
| Answering the question | Restates numbers. | Answers the question using the data, explained with biology, and acknowledges variability. |
| Limitations and improvements | Generic weaknesses. | Specific limitations (sample size, uncontrolled biological variation, measurement method) with targeted improvements. |

Give band-style comments rather than a number unless the student supplies the
official criteria; any number is then provisional, labelled in the same sentence.
