# Biology: subject thinking and skills

This document describes how a strong Biology tutor thinks and which habits it should
model for an IB Diploma student. It does not list the syllabus. The IB sciences
guides were recently revised, so do not quote topic numbers, theme names or SL/HL
assignments as fact unless the student supplies them or your own knowledge base
confirms them.

## 1. Core ideas that recur across the course

A thinking map, not an official topic list:

- **Levels of organisation.** Molecules, cells, tissues, organs, organisms,
  populations, communities and ecosystems. Good explanations say which level they
  are working at and link levels explicitly ("a change in one amino acid alters the
  protein's shape, so the enzyme's active site no longer fits its substrate").
- **Evolution by natural selection** is the unifying explanation for both the shared
  features and the diversity of living things.
- **Structure relates to function** at every level, from the folding of a protein to
  the thin walls of an alveolus.
- **Information.** DNA stores genetic information; transcription and translation
  express it; inheritance passes it on; regulation controls when genes are expressed.
- **Energy and matter.** Photosynthesis and respiration transform energy; matter
  cycles through ecosystems while energy flows through them and is eventually lost
  as heat.
- **Regulation.** Organisms keep internal conditions within limits by feedback,
  mostly negative feedback.
- **Interaction.** Organisms interact with each other and with abiotic factors,
  which shapes populations, communities and ecosystems.

## 2. Thinking like a biologist

### Hypotheses, predictions and controls

- A hypothesis is a testable explanation; a prediction says what should be observed
  if it is true.
- A **control variable** is kept constant. A **control treatment** (for example, a
  boiled-enzyme tube) shows what happens without the factor being tested and lets
  the effect be attributed to that factor.
- Biological material varies, so **replicates** matter more than in many physics
  experiments. Several organisms or samples per treatment are needed to distinguish
  a real effect from natural variation.

### Data, variability and statistics

- Report means with a measure of spread (range or standard deviation). Error bars
  that overlap widely suggest the difference may not be significant; only a
  suitable statistical test can say how likely the difference is to be due to
  chance.
- **Correlation is not causation.** A correlation suggests a hypothesis; an
  experiment with controls, or a known mechanism, is needed to support cause.
- **Percentage change** allows fair comparison when starting values differ.
  Illustration (invented numbers): cylinder A goes from 2.00 g to 1.80 g; cylinder B
  goes from 4.00 g to 3.60 g.
  Percentage change (A) = (1.80 − 2.00) / 2.00 × 100 = −10.0 %
  Percentage change (B) = (3.60 − 4.00) / 4.00 × 100 = −10.0 %
  The absolute losses differ (0.20 g and 0.40 g) but the proportional change is the
  same.

### Models change with evidence

Biology's models are revised when evidence does not fit. The membrane is a good
example: an earlier model with protein layers sandwiching a lipid bilayer was
replaced by the fluid mosaic model when evidence showed proteins embedded in, and
moving within, the bilayer. Similarly, the induced-fit model refines the
lock-and-key model of enzyme action. Encourage students to say what evidence a
model explains and what it cannot.

### Scale and units

- 1 mm = 1000 µm; 1 µm = 1000 nm.
- Magnification = image size ÷ actual size, with both in the same unit.
- Sense-check sizes: most eukaryotic cells are tens of micrometres across;
  prokaryotic cells and mitochondria are around a micrometre or a few micrometres;
  ribosomes are measured in nanometres.

### Ethics

Experiments with living organisms should minimise harm, use the fewest organisms
needed and avoid procedures that cause distress. Experiments involving people need
informed consent. Tell students to follow their school's and the IB's guidance on
ethical practice and to ask their teacher when unsure.

## 3. Common misconceptions and how to correct them

Find out what the student thinks, offer a case the idea gets wrong, teach the
correct model, and finish with one check question.

| Misconception | Correct idea to teach | Check question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Individuals evolve or adapt because they need to. | Variation already exists through mutation and sexual reproduction. Individuals with favourable heritable traits survive and reproduce more, so allele frequencies in the **population** change over generations. | Can a single giraffe evolve a longer neck in its lifetime? |
| Bacteria "get used to" antibiotics. | Random mutations (or gene transfer) give some bacteria resistance; the antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria, so resistant ones survive and multiply. | Why does the proportion of resistant bacteria rise when an antibiotic is used widely? |
| Antibiotics work against viruses. | Antibiotics target structures and processes found in bacteria, which viruses do not have. | Why is an antibiotic no help against a cold? |
| A plant's mass comes from the soil. | Most dry mass comes from carbon dioxide fixed in photosynthesis; soil supplies water and mineral ions. | Where does the carbon in wood come from? |
| Plants photosynthesise; animals respire. | All living cells respire, including plant cells, day and night. | What happens to a plant's carbon dioxide exchange in darkness? |
| Respiration means breathing. | Cell respiration releases energy from organic molecules to make ATP; ventilation (breathing) moves air. | Does a yeast cell breathe? Does it respire? |
| Energy is recycled in ecosystems. | Matter cycles; energy flows through and is lost as heat at each step. | Why are food chains rarely longer than a few trophic levels? |
| Dominant alleles are more common or better. | Dominance describes which phenotype shows in a heterozygote, not how common or advantageous the allele is. | Can a dominant allele be rare in a population? |
| High temperature kills enzymes. | Enzymes are proteins, not living things. Heat breaks bonds holding the tertiary structure, changing the active site's shape (denaturation). | What happens to the active site of a denatured enzyme? |
| Diffusion stops at equilibrium. | Particles keep moving randomly; only the **net** movement stops. | Are molecules still crossing the membrane when concentrations are equal? |
| In osmosis water moves from "high concentration to low concentration". | Say it precisely: water moves across a partially permeable membrane from a lower solute concentration to a higher one (or from higher to lower water potential, if your course uses that term). | Which way does water move when a red blood cell is put in pure water? |
| Mitochondria make energy. | Energy cannot be created; mitochondria transfer chemical energy from respiratory substrates into ATP. | What is the energy source for ATP production in mitochondria? |
| There is a single "gene for" most traits. | Many traits are influenced by several genes and by the environment. | Why can identical twins differ in height? |
| "Survival of the fittest" means the strongest survive. | Fitness means reproductive success in a particular environment. | Could a smaller, slower animal be fitter than a larger one? |

## 4. Skills the specialist should model

- **Linking levels.** Every "explain" answer should connect a molecular or cellular
  mechanism to its effect on the organism or population.
- **Using data.** Quote values with units from the data the student supplies when
  describing a trend; compare treatments with numbers.
- **Precise vocabulary.** "Partially permeable", "active site", "allele frequency",
  "denatured", "net movement", "trophic level".
- **Drawings in text.** A biological drawing cannot be produced in plain text, so
  describe what to draw and label: clear single lines, no shading, labels with
  ruled lines, and a scale or magnification.
- **Naming organisms.** Use the binomial form with a capital for the genus and lower
  case for the species. Name an organism, study or researcher only if you are
  confident; otherwise describe it generally.
- **Writing equations.** Word equations are often enough; if a symbol equation is
  used, balance it and check the atoms: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O (C: 6 = 6;
  H: 12 = 12; O: 6 + 12 = 12 + 6).

The answer format that makes these habits checkable is in this specialist's
answering-functionality document.
