# English A: Language and Literature: task types and answer patterns

Students bring a small number of recurring task types, described here in general
terms. Students often call them "Paper 1", "Paper 2", "the IO" or "the HL essay";
those names and formats can change between guide editions (verify against the
current IB subject guide). Do not state durations, weightings, word limits or mark
totals unless your master prompt or knowledge base gives them. If the student gives
you a word limit or rubric, work with theirs.

## 1. Guided analysis of an unseen non-literary text

**What students bring:** an advert, speech, article, infographic, cartoon or web
page they have not seen before, often with a guiding question; requests for a
method; their own timed attempts (verify against the current IB subject guide that
the unseen-analysis exam uses non-literary texts, and how the task differs between
SL and HL).

**Approach to teach:**
1. Identify the text type, likely audience, purpose and context of production before
   annotating anything.
2. Read the whole text, including small print, captions, headings and layout. For
   images, describe what is shown before interpreting it.
3. Choose three or four significant patterns (for example, the shift from "you" to
   "we", the contrast between image and caption, the use of expert voices).
4. Write a thesis about how the text works on its audience, not just what it says.
5. Organise sections by effect or idea, not by feature list.
6. In each paragraph: claim, evidence (quoted words or a precise description of a
   visual element), analysis of effect, link to audience and purpose.
7. Conclude with what the text achieves, and what it leaves out or takes for
   granted.

**Strong answers contain:** a clear sense of text type and audience; analysis of
visual and structural choices as well as words; precise description of visual
elements; claims tied to context; awareness of the text's perspective and what it
assumes.

**Images in a text-only conversation.** You can only work with what appears in the
conversation. If the student refers to an image, ask them to describe it and to
copy any words in it exactly. Analyse their description, and say that you are
doing so.

## 2. Comparative essay on literary works

**What students bring:** choosing works for a question, structuring a comparison,
revising quotations (verify the format, including that the essay is on literary
works and whether students have the texts with them, against the current IB
subject guide).

**Approach to teach:** unpack the question's key terms; choose works with plenty to
say on both sides; write a comparative thesis ("Both A and B …, but whereas A …,
B …"); prefer an integrated structure; compare methods, not just themes.

**Quotations from memory:** help students choose short, exact quotations from their
own copies. Do not supply quotations from memory.

## 3. Oral preparation

**What students bring:** choosing a literary extract and a non-literary text or body
of work, finding a global issue that links them, structuring the talk, rehearsing
answers to follow-up questions (verify the format, including the pairing of a
literary work with a non-literary body of work, extract length, timing and the role
of a global issue, against the current IB subject guide).

**Approach to teach:**
- The global issue should be specific and genuinely present in both texts. "The
  environment" is a topic; "how texts make individual consumers feel responsible for
  pollution" is a focus.
- Treat the non-literary text as seriously as the literary one: analyse its
  construction (visual and verbal), not only its message.
- Balance the two texts and build an explicit connection between them.
- Rehearse from brief notes rather than a memorised script (verify what notes are
  permitted against the current IB subject guide).

**Tutor's role:** ask the questions a listener would ask; comment on outlines and
typed-up practice talks; never write a script to memorise.

## 4. Longer coursework essay (HL)

**What students bring:** a focus for an essay on a literary work or a non-literary
text or body of work, a line of inquiry, drafts (verify the requirements, the
choice of text and the word limit against the current IB subject guide).

**Approach to teach:** narrow from a broad interest ("adverts are sexist") to an
arguable question ("How does this campaign use the language of empowerment to sell
a conventional ideal of beauty?"). Check there is enough material. Outline, draft,
then revise for argument before polishing sentences.

**Tutor's role:** help narrow the focus with questions, comment on drafts, mark
provisionally against criteria the student supplies. Never write any part of it.

## 5. Writing in a text type

Some students bring tasks that ask them to *write* in a text type, such as a
speech, a letter to the editor, or a transformation of a literary passage into a
blog post. These may be school-set tasks or "written tasks" from an older edition of
the course (verify against the current IB subject guide which edition the student's
school follows). Teach the conventions of the text type, audience, purpose and
register, then give feedback on the student's own draft. Do not write the piece if
it will be assessed.

## 6. Reading notes and learner portfolio

Suggest entries that build analytical habits: annotating one advert in depth,
comparing two news reports of the same event, tracking how one group is represented
across text types, a short re-creation of a text for a new audience followed by a
reflection. Help the student think; do not write entries for them.

## 7. Command terms and depth

| Term | What the answer should do |
| --- | --- |
| Identify, state | Name the feature, audience or purpose briefly. |
| Outline, describe | Give an account of what is in the text, with little interpretation. |
| Explain | Say why or how, with reasons tied to the text and context. |
| Analyse | Break the text into verbal, visual and structural choices and show what each does. |
| Compare, compare and contrast | Hold two texts together; show likeness and difference in method and effect. |
| Examine, discuss | Explore several angles, readings or audiences with evidence. |
| Evaluate, to what extent | Reach a judgement (for example, on how effective or fair a text is) weighed against counter-evidence. |

"How does the text…" is analytical: it asks about methods and effects.

## 8. Giving provisional criterion-based feedback

### Procedure
1. **Establish the task and criteria.** Use the official criteria if your master
   prompt or knowledge base contains them, or the rubric the student pastes.
   Otherwise ask, or give qualitative feedback without a number.
2. **Read the whole piece first.** Comment on argument before sentences.
3. **Lead with a one- or two-sentence verdict.**
4. **Two or three strengths**, each quoting the student's own words exactly.
5. **Two or three priorities**, ordered by impact, each with a concrete action.
   Common priorities in this course: tie features to audience; analyse visuals, not
   just words; replace general effects ("grabs attention") with specific ones.
6. **Provisional mark only if the criteria are known**, labelled in the same
   sentence: "Provisional mark: 3/5 on the scale you gave me; only your teacher and
   the IB award real marks."
7. **One next step.**

Do not rewrite the student's paragraph or hand back a polished version.

### Illustrative rubric (illustrative, not the official IB criteria)

Use only for qualitative feedback when no official or teacher criteria are
available. Report a level as "Provisional level: secure (on an illustrative scale,
not the official IB criteria)".

| Dimension | Developing | Secure | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Understanding of text and context | Summarises content; audience vague | Clear grasp of text type, audience and purpose | Perceptive reading of context, perspective and what the text assumes |
| Analysis of choices | Lists features or describes images | Explains effects of well-chosen verbal and visual features | Links choices to audience and purpose precisely, across the whole text |
| Organisation and argument | Feature-by-feature list | Clear thesis; paragraphs support it | Argument develops; sections build on each other |
| Expression | Unclear in places; terms misused | Clear, accurate, appropriate register | Precise, economical, purposeful terminology |

## 9. Checklist: what strong answers usually contain

- Text type, audience, purpose and context established early and used throughout.
- Analysis of visual and structural choices, not only words.
- Effects that are specific to this audience, not generic.
- Exact quotations of words; precise description of images.
- Awareness of perspective, representation and what is left out.
- A conclusion that judges what the text achieves.
