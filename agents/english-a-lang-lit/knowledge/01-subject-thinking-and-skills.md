# English A: Language and Literature: subject thinking and skills

This is background for the tutor, not text to show students. It sits under your
master prompt and your own knowledge base. General answering rules shared by every
specialist are in the shared answering document; this file covers the subject.

## 1. What the course asks students to do

English A: Language and Literature is for students who read and write English at a
high level. It studies **literary works and non-literary texts** side by side, and
asks how language, image and form make meaning in context. Non-literary texts come
in many **text types**: advertisements, speeches, opinion columns, news reports,
interviews, infographics, cartoons, photographs, posters, blogs, social media posts,
letters, manifestos, travel writing and more.

Students learn to:

- analyse how texts are constructed for particular audiences and purposes;
- explain how meaning depends on context, both where a text was made and where it
  is read;
- examine how texts represent people, places and ideas, and whose perspective they
  serve;
- compare texts, literary and non-literary, and connect them with wider issues;
- write and speak about texts clearly, with organised, well-evidenced argument.

Like the Literature course, it is framed by a few broad lenses: roughly, how readers,
writers and texts make meaning; how time and place shape texts; and how texts
connect with one another, with recurring concepts such as identity, perspective and
representation (verify the current names of these areas and concepts against the
current IB subject guide).

## 2. How this course differs from English A: Literature

Use this as a heuristic for spotting which course a student takes. The details are
course-design facts that can change between guide editions, so verify each row
against the current IB subject guide before stating it as fact to a student.

| | English A: Literature | English A: Language and Literature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Texts studied | Literary works only | Literary works and non-literary texts across many text types |
| Typical unseen analysis | A literary passage | A non-literary text |
| Comparative essay | Literary works | Literary works |
| Oral | Extracts from literary works | Commonly a literary work paired with a non-literary body of work |
| Typical vocabulary | Form, genre, voice, imagery | All of that, plus text type, audience, purpose, register, multimodality, rhetoric, representation |

When a student mentions adverts, "text types" or a "body of work", they are very
likely in this course. When they mention only poems and novels, they could be in
either.

## 3. Core concepts

- **Text type and conventions.** Each text type has expected features: a news
  report has a headline, lead paragraph and attributed quotations; a charity appeal
  has a problem, an emotional focus and a call to action. Writers follow
  conventions, and sometimes break them for effect.
- **Audience.** The target audience (who the text is aimed at) and the implied
  reader (the kind of person the text assumes you are). Texts *position* readers: a
  text that says "we all know…" invites agreement before argument.
- **Purpose.** Usually layered. An advert may inform, entertain and build a brand
  identity while it persuades.
- **Context.** Of production (who made it, when, where, in what medium, under what
  pressures) and of reception (how different audiences, then and now, read it).
- **Register and tone.** Formality, intimacy, authority; shifts in register are
  often the most revealing moments.
- **Multimodality.** Words, images, layout, colour and typography working together,
  or against each other.
- **Representation.** How people, groups and places are portrayed; whose voices are
  present and whose are missing.
- **Ideology and power.** The values a text takes for granted. Ask what it treats
  as normal.
- **Rhetoric.** Appeals to credibility (ethos), emotion (pathos) and reasoning
  (logos), and timing (kairos).

## 4. A framework: context, construction, consequence

An original, portable framework for any text, literary or not:

1. **Context.** Who made this, for whom, when, where, in what medium, and why?
2. **Construction.** What verbal, visual and structural choices were made?
3. **Consequence.** What effect do those choices have on the audience? What view of
   the world does the text promote, and whose interests does it serve?

### Verbal features worth noticing
Pronouns (inclusive "we", direct "you"); imperatives; modal verbs ("must",
"could"); lexical fields; connotation; nominalisation and the passive voice, which
can hide who did what ("mistakes were made"); hyperbole; statistics and expert
voices; rhetorical questions; lists of three; juxtaposition; headlines and
subheadings; shifts of register.

### Visual and layout features worth noticing
The reading path (where the eye goes first and why); salience (what is biggest,
brightest, most central); framing and distance (a close-up suggests intimacy, a long
shot context or isolation); gaze (a figure looking at the viewer makes a demand; a
figure looking away offers itself to be observed); colour palette; typography; white
space; the relationship between image and caption (the caption often fixes how we
read the image).

## 5. Worked illustration (invented advertisement)

> **Invented advertisement, described for this document.** A full-page magazine
> advert. The top two-thirds show a close-up of a teenager's hands holding a dented
> metal water bottle covered in travel stickers. The bottom third is white, with
> one line of black, lower-case text: "this one's been everywhere with me." Below
> it, in smaller type: "Made to last. Made to refill." A small logo sits in the
> bottom right corner.

- **Context.** A magazine advert for a reusable product, aimed at young readers who
  care about travel and sustainability.
- **Construction.** The dents and stickers are signs of a life lived, so the bottle
  is presented as a companion and a record of identity rather than a container. The
  close-up of hands places the viewer in the owner's position. The lower-case,
  first-person caption imitates a personal, informal voice, as if a friend were
  speaking. The slogan's two short parallel fragments ("Made to last. Made to
  refill.") sound like a promise.
- **Consequence.** Buying is reframed as an expression of values and personality.
  There is a tension worth discussing: the advert sells a new product by promoting
  consuming less.

Notice that the analysis never says "the advert uses colour to attract attention".
Every feature is tied to a specific effect on this audience.

## 6. Literary works in this course

Literary works are read with the same close-reading skills as in the Literature
course: voice, form, structure, imagery, context. The difference is often in the
connections students draw. A Dickens novel's presentation of poverty might be
discussed alongside a modern charity appeal, asking how each positions the reader
towards people in need.

## 7. Common misconceptions and how to correct them

| Misconception | How to correct it |
| --- | --- |
| "Non-literary texts are simple; the meaning is obvious." | Show how one small choice (a pronoun, a crop of a photo) changes the meaning. Obvious messages are often the most carefully built. |
| "Analysis means listing persuasive techniques." | Select the two or three most significant features and explain how each works on this audience in this context. Acronym checklists are for noticing, not for writing. |
| "The purpose is to persuade." | Ask: persuade whom, of what, and by positioning them how? Purposes are usually layered. |
| "Describing an image is analysing it." | Every visual description needs a "which suggests…" or "which positions the viewer…". |
| "Colours have fixed meanings: red means danger." | Meaning depends on context. Red might signal danger, passion, a brand or a political party. Ground every claim in the text. |
| "Bias means the text is bad or wrong." | All texts have a perspective. Analyse how it is constructed; evaluate fairness separately and with evidence. |
| "The audience is everyone." | Identify the target audience and the reader the text constructs. Even public notices have an implied reader. |
| "Context is a background paragraph." | Use context where it explains a specific choice: why this image, this word, this moment. |
| "Statistics in the text are facts I should repeat." | Treat them as rhetorical choices. You usually cannot check them; analyse how they are selected and framed. |
| "Literary and non-literary texts need completely different skills." | The core move is the same: choice, effect, meaning in context. Only some of the vocabulary changes. |

**Correcting well.** Name the habit kindly, show one improved version on a
different (invented) text, then ask the student to revise their own sentence. Do
not rewrite their work for them.
