# Physics: task types and answer patterns

This document describes the kinds of request a Physics student brings and the
answer pattern that works for each. It does not describe the official paper
structure, timings or mark totals. If those matter to a student, tell them to check
the current subject guide or their teacher.

## 1. Multiple-choice reasoning

**What students ask:** "Why is the answer C?" or "How do I do these faster?"

**Approach**

1. Identify the physics principle before looking at the options.
2. Predict the answer, or its form, from that principle.
3. Eliminate options with quick tests: units and dimensions, limiting cases (what
   happens if a mass becomes very large or an angle becomes 0°), sign and direction,
   and order of magnitude.
4. Explain why the correct option is right **and** why each distractor is wrong. A
   bare letter teaches nothing.

**Example of a dimension check.** Which expression could give the period of a
mass–spring oscillator: 2π√(m/k) or 2π√(k/m)? The units of m/k are
kg ÷ (N m⁻¹) = kg ÷ (kg m s⁻² m⁻¹) = kg ÷ (kg s⁻²) = s². So √(m/k) has units of s,
and 2π√(m/k) is the only option that can be a time.

Whether a calculator is allowed in a multiple-choice paper is a course-structure fact;
do not state it unless your own knowledge base confirms it (verify against the
current IB subject guide).

## 2. Short and structured answers

**Approach**

- Read the command term and the mark allocation together. Marks are a guide to the
  number of distinct physics points expected, not a word count.
- Make each point one physics idea, applied to the situation in the question.
  "Friction acts" earns little; "friction between the tyres and the road provides
  the centripetal force" does the job.
- Keep the chain explicit for "explain": cause → physics principle → effect.

**What strong answers contain**

- Correct technical vocabulary used precisely.
- Quantities with units and directions where relevant.
- A final sentence that answers the question as asked.

## 3. Data-based questions

**Approach**

1. Read the axis labels, units and scales before describing anything.
2. Describe the trend in words, then quantify it (gradient, intercept, a ratio or a
   percentage change).
3. Link the gradient or intercept to a physical quantity, including its units.
4. Comment on anomalies, scatter and uncertainty bars. A best-fit line need not
   pass through every point; a line that passes through all the error bars
   supports the model.
5. For linearised graphs, say what straight line the model predicts and whether
   the data match it.

**Never invent data.** If a student refers to a graph or table they have not pasted,
explain the method and ask for the values. See the worked examples document for this
case.

## 4. Calculations

**Pattern (use every time)**

1. Givens: symbol, value, unit, converted to SI on its own line.
2. Relation in symbols.
3. Rearrangement in symbols.
4. Substitution with every number visible.
5. Result with unit and significant figures, with a sentence on the s.f. choice.
6. Sense check: units, size, sign.

**Example (numbers invented for teaching).** A wire of length 2.0 m and diameter
0.50 mm has a resistance of 4.0 Ω. Find the resistivity of its material.

Given: L = 2.0 m; d = 0.50 mm = 0.50 × 10⁻³ m; R = 4.0 Ω
Relation: R = ρL / A, with A = πd² / 4
Rearranged: ρ = RA / L
A = π(0.50 × 10⁻³)² / 4 = 1.963 × 10⁻⁷ m²
ρ = (4.0)(1.963 × 10⁻⁷) / (2.0) = 3.93 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m (unrounded)
Result: ρ = 3.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m (2 s.f., matching the data)
Units check: Ω × m² ÷ m = Ω m.

The most common error here is using the diameter instead of the radius, which makes
the area four times too large. Point this out when it happens.

## 5. Extended responses ("explain", "discuss", "evaluate")

**Approach**

- State the key physics claim first.
- Build the chain of reasoning in short linked steps.
- Bring in a relevant equation when it sharpens the argument, and say what each
  symbol stands for.
- For "evaluate" or "discuss", weigh at least two considerations and reach a
  judgement.

**Example chain.** Why does the temperature of a gas rise when it is compressed
quickly? Work is done on the gas by the piston → there is little time for thermal
energy transfer to the surroundings → the work done increases the internal energy of
the gas → for an ideal gas, internal energy is the total random kinetic energy of the
particles → the average kinetic energy rises → temperature rises.

## 6. Practical work and internal assessment guidance

The specialist helps a student plan, check and improve their own investigation. It
never writes text for submission (see this specialist's answering-functionality
document for the full integrity rules).

Useful guidance:

- A focused research question names one independent variable, one dependent
  variable and the system studied.
- Variables: say how each control variable is kept constant and why it matters.
- Data: enough distinct values over a sensible range, with repeats where readings
  vary; record raw data with units and uncertainties.
- Processing: linearise where possible; show one sample calculation clearly; derive
  uncertainties from the measurement method, not by guessing.
- Evaluation: separate random from systematic effects; propose realistic,
  specific improvements tied to an identified weakness.
- Safety and ethics: electrical, thermal, radiation and laser hazards; disposal of
  materials.

Official IA criterion names, weightings and length limits are not stated here.
Verify them against the current IB subject guide.

## 7. Feedback on drafts and answers

- Quote or point to the student's own words or line of working.
- Fix physics errors first, then communication, then presentation.
- Give two or three priorities, each with the change that would improve the work.
- If you award a mark, label it provisional in the same sentence.

## 8. Command-term depth

These descriptions are paraphrases for tutoring. The official definitions are in the
current subject guide.

| Command term | What the answer must do |
| --- | --- |
| State, identify | Give a specific name, value or fact with no explanation. |
| Outline | Give a brief account of the main points. |
| Describe | Give a detailed account of features or a trend, without causes. |
| Explain | Give reasons or a mechanism: why or how something happens. |
| Calculate | Reach a numerical answer and show the working. |
| Determine | Reach a single answer by calculation or graph reading, with working. |
| Estimate | Give an approximate value with sensible reasoning and rounding. |
| Deduce | Reach a conclusion that follows logically from the information given. |
| Derive | Manipulate relations to reach a stated result, step by step. |
| Show that | Give enough working that each step is checkable; give the answer to one more significant figure than the stated value where possible. |
| Sketch | Draw a graph or diagram showing the general shape and key features, with labelled axes. |
| Suggest | Propose a reasonable answer where several may be valid. |
| Compare | Give similarities and differences, referring to both items throughout. |
| Discuss, evaluate | Weigh strengths and limitations and reach a judgement. |
| Justify | Give evidence or reasoning supporting a choice or conclusion. |

## 9. Provisional criterion-based feedback

**On exam-style questions with a stated mark allocation**

- Build illustrative mark points from the physics, explain which ones the student
  earned, and say what is missing.
- Label any number provisional in the same sentence, for example: "Provisional mark:
  2 out of 3 — only your teacher and the IB award real marks."
- If no mark allocation is given, give qualitative feedback or ask for the
  allocation.

**On investigation drafts**

Use the illustrative rubric below only when the student has not supplied their
official criteria. It is illustrative, not the official IB criteria.

| Aspect (illustrative) | Developing | Secure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | Question is broad or names no measurable variable. | Question names one independent and one dependent variable in a defined system. |
| Method | Control variables listed without how or why. | Each control variable has a method and a reason. |
| Data and processing | Few values, no uncertainties, unclear sample calculation. | Sufficient range, uncertainties propagated, linearised graph with gradient and its uncertainty. |
| Conclusion | Restates data without linking to the physics. | Uses the gradient or result to answer the question, compared with the model or an accepted value, with uncertainty taken into account. |
| Evaluation | Generic ("more repeats"). | Identifies specific systematic and random effects and gives targeted improvements. |

Give band-style feedback ("this is closer to Developing for method because …")
rather than a number, unless the student supplies the official criteria. Any number
you then give is provisional, labelled in the same sentence.
